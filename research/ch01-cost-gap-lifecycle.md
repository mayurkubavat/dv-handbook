# Ch. 1 research note: cost, gap, lifecycle

Every claim below carries an inline citation of the form `[Source, YYYY-MM-DD](URL)` and, where a `refs.bib` key exists, `[@key]`. Figures that could not be verified against a primary source are marked **UNVERIFIED** and listed again in Section 7.

## 1. Verification effort share and staffing (Wilson Research Group / Siemens studies)

The Wilson Research Group (WRG) functional verification study is a biennial, Siemens-commissioned industry survey. Harry Foster authors the reports and republishes the findings as a blog series on Verification Horizons. The 2020 report is freely mirrored as a PDF; the 2022 findings are available in full only through the blog series; the 2024 report is behind a registration wall, so 2024 figures below come from Foster's own 2025 blog post, the Siemens landing page, and Ed Sperling's DVCon-based reporting.

**Share of project time in verification.** Neither the 2020 nor the 2022 edition states a single mean. Both present a histogram (Fig. 7 in 2020, Fig. 8-1 in 2022) with two extremes: projects that integrate mostly pre-verified IP spend little time in verification, and projects with much new IP spend a lot. The 2020 report notes "the increase in project times greater than 60 percent for this year's study" [Siemens/WRG 2020 IC/ASIC trend report, 2020-10-13](https://uobdv.github.io/Design-Verification/WilsonResearchGroupFunctionalVerificationStudy/2020-WRGFV-Study/ic-asic-trend-report_2020-wilson-research-verification-study_hfoster.pdf) [@foster2020wrg]. Foster's 2025 blog summarises the study data as "60–70% of engineering effort in chip projects belongs in verification" [Foster, Verification Horizons, 2025-09-03](https://blogs.sw.siemens.com/verificationhorizons/2025/09/03/why-first-silicon-success-is-getting-harder-for-system-companies/) [@foster2025firstsilicon]. That sentence is the most citable modern form of the "70%" claim, but note it is a summary by the study's author, not a tabulated statistic.

**Verifier-to-designer ratio.** 2020: "across all market segments, we find about a one-to-one ratio in terms of mean peak number of verification and design engineers. However, in some market segments, such as processors, it is not unusual to find a 5-to-1 ratio." Demand for verification engineers grew at a 6.8% CAGR from 2007 to 2020 versus about 3% for design engineers [Siemens/WRG 2020, 2020-10-13](https://uobdv.github.io/Design-Verification/WilsonResearchGroupFunctionalVerificationStudy/2020-WRGFV-Study/ic-asic-trend-report_2020-wilson-research-verification-study_hfoster.pdf) [@foster2020wrg]. 2022: same one-to-one statement; CAGRs restated as 6.2% (verification) versus 2.7% (design) for 2007 to 2022 [Foster, WRG 2022 Part 8, 2022-12-12](https://blogs.sw.siemens.com/verificationhorizons/2022/12/12/part-8-the-2022-wilson-research-group-functional-verification-study/) [@foster2022wrg]. 2024 ratio: not retrievable without registration (gap).

**Where engineers spend their time.** Design engineers spent 47% of their time on verification in 2020 and 49% in 2022 [@foster2020wrg; @foster2022wrg]. In both editions verification engineers spend more time debugging than on any other activity (the reports give a pie chart; no single percentage is stated in the text).

**First-silicon success and respins.**

| Edition | First-silicon success | Behind schedule | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2020 | about 32% | 68% | [WRG 2020 report, 2020-10-13](https://uobdv.github.io/Design-Verification/WilsonResearchGroupFunctionalVerificationStudy/2020-WRGFV-Study/ic-asic-trend-report_2020-wilson-research-verification-study_hfoster.pdf) [@foster2020wrg] |
| 2022 | about 24% ("lowest ... in the past twenty years") | 66% | [Foster, WRG 2022 Part 12, 2023-01-09](https://blogs.sw.siemens.com/verificationhorizons/2023/01/09/part-12-the-2022-wilson-research-group-functional-verification-study/) [@foster2022wrg] |
| 2024 | 14% ("lowest figure in more than twenty years") | 75% | [Sperling, Semiconductor Engineering, 2025-03-27](https://semiengineering.com/first-time-silicon-success-plummets/) [@sperling2025plummets]; [Siemens 2024 report landing page, accessed 2026-09-06](https://resources.sw.siemens.com/en-US/white-paper-2024-wilson-research-group-ic-asic-functional-verification-trend-report/) [@foster2024wrg] |

Foster's framing of the long-run baseline: "Historically, for the past 20 years, about 30% of all IC/ASIC projects were able to achieve first silicon success" [@foster2022wrg]. The 2020 report adds that the number of required spins before production "has not increased" from 2012 to 2020 despite complexity growth [@foster2020wrg].

**Share of respins due to logic/functional bugs.** All three editions say "logic or functional flaws" remain the leading cause of respins, but the text does not state the percentage; it is read off a bar chart (Fig. 4 in 2020, Fig. 12-3 in 2022). 2020 also reports a jump in respins attributed to tuning analog circuits (41%), and first tracked safety (11%) and security (10%) flaws [@foster2020wrg]. 2022 confirms the analog spike persisted [@foster2022wrg]. For 2024, Synopsys's Frank Schirrmeister is quoted saying "logic function is still the most prominent issue" and that "70% of respins are design errors due to changes in the spec"; that second number is his paraphrase of a chart and should not be quoted as a WRG statistic [Sperling, 2025-03-27](https://semiengineering.com/first-time-silicon-success-plummets/) [@sperling2025plummets]. Root cause of functional flaws: in both 2020 and 2022, "design errors are the leading cause of functional flaws, and the situation is worsening," with changing, incorrect, and incomplete specifications a recurring theme [@foster2020wrg; @foster2022wrg].

**Trend across editions.** First-silicon success fell from roughly 30% (long-run) to 32% (2020), 24% (2022), 14% (2024); behind-schedule share moved 68% to 66% to 75%. Foster attributes the 2024 drop partly to system companies (automotive OEMs, hyperscalers, consumer brands) entering chip design without mature verification practice [@foster2025firstsilicon], and calls the situation "Productivity Gap 2.0" [@sperling2025plummets].

## 2. Cost of a bug by stage

**The "10x per stage" rule has no hardware primary source.** The closest documented source is the software-engineering literature. The NIST-commissioned RTI report of 2002 presents an *example* table (explicitly labelled "Example Only") of relative repair cost by stage found: 1X at requirements/architecture, 5X at coding/unit test, 10X at integration/system test, 15X at beta, 30X post-release, and traces the idea to Boehm (1976) and a 1995 Baziuk study [NIST/RTI, "The Economic Impacts of Inadequate Infrastructure for Software Testing", 2002-05-29](https://www.nist.gov/system/files/documents/director/planning/report02-3.pdf) [@nist2002testing]. For the chapter: present the escalation as a well-supported *qualitative* pattern with software-engineering provenance, and say plainly that no published silicon dataset backs a fixed multiplier.

**Design and mask cost at advanced nodes.** IBS figures as reported by Semiconductor Engineering: total IC design cost of $51.3M at 28nm, $297.8M at 7nm, $542.2M at 5nm, and $500M to $1.5B at 3nm (the $1.5B case being a complex Nvidia GPU) [LaPedus, "Big Trouble At 3nm", 2018-06-21](https://semiengineering.com/big-trouble-at-3nm/) [@lapedus2018bigtrouble]. Earlier IBS-based reporting cautioned that headline leading-edge figures (up to $300M in 2014) overstate what most teams spend, which is "generally between $20 million and $50 million" with heavy IP reuse [Sperling, "How Much Will That Chip Cost?", 2014-03-27](https://semiengineering.com/how-much-will-that-chip-cost/). For the mask set itself, the only citable statement found this run is Foster's: "At 3nm, a mask set costs tens of millions of dollars," and "at today's advanced nodes, a single respin can wipe out an entire product cycle" [@foster2025firstsilicon]. Foster's 2022 write-up likewise ties the falling first-silicon rate to "increasing wafer and mask cost" [@foster2022wrg]. **UNVERIFIED**: specific per-node mask-set prices (for example "$30M at 3nm") circulate widely but no foundry statement or IBS table was retrieved; do not print a number without one.

**Time-to-market cost, citable example.** Intel's 6 Series chipset (Cougar Point) is the cleanest public case: Intel reported a total repair-and-replacement cost of $733M in its FY2011 10-K, booked as $311M in Q4 2010, $343M in Q1 2011 and $79M in Q2 2011, with shipments of the fixed stepping starting February 2011 [Intel Form 10-K FY2011, Note 20, 2012-02-23](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/50863/000119312512075534/d302695d10k.htm) [@intel2011cougarpoint]. The commonly quoted "$700M plus $300M of lost revenue" comes from Intel's press release of 2011-01-31, which was not retrievable this run (archive.org has no copy of that newsroom URL). The ITRS 2007 design chapter offers the structural framing: without design-technology innovation, hardware design cost for its 2007 consumer SoC driver would have been about $900M instead of $15.5M, and software cost exceeded hardware design cost for the first time in 2007 [ITRS 2007 Design, 2007](https://www.semiconductors.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/08/2007Design.pdf) [@itrs2007design].

## 3. Famous escapes

### Pentium FDIV, 1994 (functional/logic)
- **Mechanism.** The Pentium's SRT divider used a PLA lookup table of quotient digits; a scripting error while downloading the table into the PLA omitted five entries, so certain divisor/remainder pairs read the wrong quotient digit [Sharangpani and Barton, Intel white paper, 1994-11-30](https://www.ardent-tool.com/CPU/Intel/fdiv/white11.pdf) [@sharangpani1994fdiv]; Pratt gives the detailed reconstruction, including Tim Coe's model and the worst known case 4195835/3145727 with relative error 2^-14 [Pratt, "Anatomy of the Pentium Bug", 1995](https://www5.in.tum.de/~huckle/anapentium) [@pratt1995fdiv].
- **How it was found.** Pratt: "The error was first noticed within Intel and independently by Prof. Thomas Nicely at Lynchburg College, Virginia, in the course of an ongoing project to estimate the sum of the reciprocals of the twin primes. Nicely publicized his discovery at the end of October [1994]" [@pratt1995fdiv]. So: escaped pre-silicon verification, found by a customer running long numerical computations, on shipped silicon.
- **Intel's risk framing.** Intel's white paper estimated 1 in 9 billion random divides would be wrong and that a typical spreadsheet user would hit it once in 27,000 years; the empirical basis was over a trillion random samples [@sharangpani1994fdiv]. Pratt notes Intel initially tried to restrict replacements before moving to no-questions-asked exchange [@pratt1995fdiv].
- **Cost.** A $475M pre-tax charge in Q4 1994 [Intel Form 8-K, 1995-01-17](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0000050863/000005086395000001/0000050863-95-000001.txt) [@intel1995fdiv].

### Intel 6 Series "Cougar Point" SATA, 2011 (circuit/electrical, not logic)
- **Classification.** A transistor-level design issue, not a functional bug: Intel's 10-K calls it a "design issue" fixed with "a silicon fix" [@intel2011cougarpoint]. Press accounts of Intel's 2011-01-31 briefing describe a transistor in the 3 Gb/s SATA PLL clock tree biased at too high a voltage, causing leakage-driven degradation over time in ports 2 to 5; **UNVERIFIED** this run (AnandTech and the Intel newsroom page were not retrievable), so attribute to "Intel's January 2011 briefing as reported in the trade press" if used.
- **How it was found.** "As part of our ongoing quality assurance procedures" after shipment began in Q4 2010 [@intel2011cougarpoint], i.e. post-silicon reliability testing, not pre-silicon verification.
- **Cost.** $733M total (see Section 2) [@intel2011cougarpoint].

### AMD Phenom / Barcelona TLB erratum 298, 2007 (functional/logic, coherence)
- **Mechanism.** AMD Family 10h erratum 298, "L2 Eviction May Occur During Processor Operation To Set Accessed or Dirty Bit": the operation that sets the accessed or dirty bit of a page-table entry in L2 "may not be atomic," leaving a window in which a stale entry can be installed in L3 before the modified copy returns to L2; a probe in that window can lose coherency or corrupt data for an unrelated cached operation. Potential effects: L3 protocol machine check, loss of coherency on a page-table cache line, data corruption. Workaround: BIOS sets HWCR[TlbCacheDis] and MSRC001_1023[1]; "Fix Planned: Yes" [AMD, Revision Guide for AMD Family 10h Processors, pub. 41322 rev. 3.92, 2012-03 (archived copy)](https://web.archive.org/web/2019/https://www.amd.com/system/files/TechDocs/41322_10h_Rev_Gd.pdf) [@amd2007tlb].
- **Classification.** A logic/microarchitectural correctness bug in cache-coherence handling of page-table updates: exactly the class pre-silicon DV is meant to catch.
- **How found / cost.** **UNVERIFIED**: AMD's own account of discovery (reported in late 2007 as found during internal validation with customer reports of hangs under virtualisation), the shipment delay of Barcelona/Phenom until the B3 stepping in 2008, and the roughly 10 to 20% performance loss from the BIOS workaround were all widely reported but no primary AMD financial or discovery statement was retrieved. Use the revision guide for the mechanism and label the rest as press reporting.

### Intel Skylake/Kaby Lake hyper-threading erratum, 2017 (functional/logic)
- **Mechanism and scope.** Errata SKZ7/SKW144/SKL150/SKX150/KBL095/KBW095: with hyper-threading enabled, the processor could "dangerously misbehave," causing "spurious errors ... data corruption, and data loss." Fixed by microcode; the workaround was to disable hyper-threading [Holschuh, debian-devel, 2017-06-25](https://lists.debian.org/debian-devel/2017/06/msg00308.html) [@holschuh2017skl150].
- **How it was found.** Users, not the vendor: OCaml compiler developers had seen malfunctions since Q2 2016, investigated from 2017-01-06, narrowed the trigger to Skylake with hyper-threading, and on 2017-05-29 Mark Shinwell matched the symptom to a microcode changelog entry and confirmed the fix [@holschuh2017skl150]. Time from first symptom to public warning: about a year.
- **Cost.** No public financial impact; microcode-fixable.

### Zenbleed, CVE-2023-20593, 2023 (microarchitectural / speculative-execution data leak, fixed in microcode)
- **Mechanism.** Ormandy: on Zen 2, a mispredicted `vzeroupper` is recovered incorrectly. `vzeroupper` sets a "z-bit" in the register allocation table and frees the upper YMM physical register; if the instruction is squashed after a branch mispredict, simply clearing the z-bit is not enough, producing "a use-after-free in a CPU." Triggering requires the XMM register merge optimization, a register rename, and a mispredicted `vzeroupper` in a precise window. Because the physical register file is shared per core (including across SMT siblings), stale YMM contents from other processes, VMs, or containers become readable; the exploit leaked about 30 kB per core per second [Ormandy, "Zenbleed", 2023-07-24](https://lock.cmpxchg8b.com/zenbleed.html) [@ormandy2023zenbleed]. AMD's description: "a register in 'Zen 2' CPUs may not be written to 0 correctly," allowing data from another process or thread to be stored in the YMM register; severity Medium [AMD-SB-7008, 2023-07-24](https://www.amd.com/en/resources/product-security/bulletin/amd-sb-7008.html) [@amd2023sb7008].
- **Affected products.** All Zen 2: Ryzen 3000, PRO 3000, Threadripper 3000, Ryzen 4000 and PRO 4000 with Radeon graphics, Ryzen 5000 mobile with Radeon graphics, Ryzen 7020, and EPYC "Rome" (7002) [@ormandy2023zenbleed; @amd2023sb7008].
- **Timeline.** Reported to AMD 2023-05-15; public disclosure and AMD bulletin 2023-07-24; EPYC Rome microcode 0x0830107B dated 2023-06-06; client AGESA firmware fixes landed between 2023-11 and 2024-04 per the bulletin's revision history [@ormandy2023zenbleed; @amd2023sb7008].
- **How it was found.** "I found this bug by fuzzing" on real silicon. Ormandy used hardware performance counters as coverage feedback for the fuzzer and an "Oracle Serialization" technique: generate a random instruction sequence, produce a serialized variant with fences and alignment changes, and compare final architectural state; a mismatch signals a microarchitectural error. He explicitly frames this as an outsider's take on post-silicon validation: "vendors fuzz their own products extensively - the industry term for it is Post-Silicon Validation. So how come this bug wasn't found earlier?" [@ormandy2023zenbleed]. No time-to-find is stated.
- **Mitigation.** Microcode update (preferred), or the chicken bit DE_CFG[9] via MSR 0xC0011029 with some performance cost; disabling SMT is not sufficient [@ormandy2023zenbleed].
- **Classification.** A functional correctness bug in speculative-recovery logic (the register is architecturally required to be zero) with security consequences; it sits between "logic bug" and "side channel" because the leak is architectural, not timing-based.

### Intel 13th/14th Gen "Raptor Lake" instability, 2024 (electrical/voltage, not logic)
Intel's root cause: "Vmin Shift Instability," localized to a clock-tree circuit inside the core that is subject to reliability aging under elevated voltage and temperature, with a microcode-based mitigation (0x12B) limiting voltage requests [Intel Community, 2024-09](https://community.intel.com/t5/Blogs/Tech-Innovation/Client/Intel-Core-13th-and-14th-Gen-Desktop-Instability-Root-Cause/post/1633239) [@intel2024raptorlake]. Classify as electrical/reliability. Found in the field by end users and system builders reporting crashes, then root-caused by Intel.

## 4. Definitions and history

**Verification vs validation vs test.** IEEE 1012-2024 is a process standard covering system, software, and hardware life cycles; "V&V processes determine whether the development products of a given activity conform to the requirements of that activity and whether the product satisfies its intended use and user needs," and may include "analysis, evaluation, review, inspection, assessment, and testing." Approved 2024-11-12, published 2025-08-22, superseding 1012-2016 [IEEE SA page for 1012-2024, accessed 2026-09-06](https://standards.ieee.org/ieee/1012/7324/) [@ieee1012-2024]. The first clause ("conform to the requirements of that activity") is verification; the second ("satisfies its intended use") is validation. Industry usage in silicon follows the same split loosely: "verification" is pre-silicon checking of the design against specification; "validation" is what is done on real silicon (Mishra et al. title their tutorial "Post-Silicon Validation") [@mishra2017postsilicon]; "test" is manufacturing test for defects. The full standard text is paywalled; only the scope was verified.

**Origin of "design productivity gap" and "verification gap".** The ITRS 2007 design chapter: "Recent editions of the ITRS have noted a design productivity gap—the number of available transistors growing faster than the ability to meaningfully design them," and lists as its first cross-cutting challenge "verification, which is a bottleneck that has now reached crisis proportions." Its cost model assumes "the percentage of the design effort spent on verification has a linear trend starting at 70% in 2007 and ending at 50% in 2022" [@itrs2007design]. Foster's ICCAD 2013 paper argues that the predicted design productivity gap "never happened" because reuse and abstraction absorbed it [@foster2013gap]; in 2025 he calls today's situation "Productivity Gap 2.0" [@sperling2025plummets].

**Canonical books, one line each.** Bergeron's *Writing Testbenches* (2nd ed., 2003) established the self-checking, constrained-random, coverage-driven testbench as the object of verification engineering [@bergeron2003testbenches]. Wile, Goss and Roesner's *Comprehensive Functional Verification* (2005) frames verification as a complete industry cycle from plan through regression to post-silicon, drawing on IBM practice [@wile2005cfv]. Neither was consulted in full this run; use them for framing, not numbers.

**The "70% of effort is verification" claim.** Goering's 2004 EE Times survey of 662 chip-design respondents found functional verification took 22% of the design cycle when the cycle is defined from system-level design through physical verification; he called the 70% figure "more myth than science," while conceding that within the RTL phase alone verification "might take half the effort, or more" [Goering, EE Times, 2004-06-14](https://www.eetimes.com/is-verification-really-70-percent/) [@goering2004seventy]. The WRG studies never state 70% as a single number; they show a distribution of "percentage of project time spent in verification" and note growth in the over-60% bucket [@foster2020wrg]. Foster's 2025 summary, "60–70% of engineering effort ... belongs in verification," is the closest authored statement [@foster2025firstsilicon]. The ITRS 2007 model used 70% as its 2007 starting assumption [@itrs2007design].

## 5. Lifecycle and sign-off

DV's position in the flow, as the sources describe it: specification and architecture, then RTL design, then pre-silicon functional verification (simulation, formal, emulation/FPGA prototyping), then synthesis and physical design to tapeout, then post-silicon validation on first samples, then production test. Mishra, Morad, Ziv and Ray's tutorial defines post-silicon validation as the activity on fabricated silicon that catches what pre-silicon missed, covering functional and electrical bugs, and emphasises its limited observability compared with simulation [@mishra2017postsilicon] (abstract only verified; article paywalled). The WRG studies enumerate the pre-silicon techniques and their adoption: simulation-based methods, formal property checking (4.2% CAGR since 2012) and automatic formal apps (6% CAGR), and emulation or FPGA prototyping, with emulation overtaking FPGA prototyping for designs above roughly 1B gates [Foster, WRG 2022 Part 9, 2022-12-18](https://blogs.sw.siemens.com/verificationhorizons/2022/12/18/part-9-the-2022-wilson-research-group-functional-verification-study/) [@foster2022wrg]. UVM is the predominant testbench methodology and SystemVerilog Assertions the predominant assertion language [Foster, WRG 2022 Part 10, 2022-12-26](https://blogs.sw.siemens.com/verificationhorizons/2022/12/26/part-10-the-2022-wilson-research-group-functional-verification-study/) [@foster2022wrg].

**Sign-off.** The Verification Academy Coverage Cookbook organises closure around an executable test plan traced from specification to functional coverage, with code coverage and functional coverage as the measured metrics [Verification Academy, Coverage Cookbook, accessed 2026-09-06](https://verificationacademy.com/cookbook/coverage/) (page body is script-rendered; only the table of contents was verified). Foster describes mature teams as having "muscle memory around flows, sign-off criteria, and coverage closure" [@foster2025firstsilicon]. For the chapter, state sign-off as comprising coverage closure (code and functional against the plan), regression health (a clean, stable regression over the final RTL), bug convergence (open-rate falling to zero with no open blockers), and a waiver review of uncovered or excluded items; attribute the list to the cookbook plus Wile et al., and do not attach percentages to it. **UNVERIFIED**: no DVCon paper giving a canonical sign-off checklist was retrieved.
