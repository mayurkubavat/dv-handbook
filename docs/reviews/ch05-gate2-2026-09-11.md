# Chapter 5 gate 2: The SystemVerilog Testbench Language, first pass

Scope, in the brief's order: every technical claim reproduced with
SystemVerilog written for this review and run under both simulators;
every spliced `run.out` compared byte for byte with a fresh
`scripts/check-examples.sh ch05`; the support table's probes read one by
one against their labels and the docstring's definitions; every citation
new to this chapter fetched from the entry's own URL and read against the
sentence that carries it; originality; house rules; the rendered PDF
pages for the table and the two-tool output; clarity and the line with
the next chapter.

Toolchain: Icarus Verilog 13.0 (stable), Verilator 5.030 2024-10-27,
poppler, conda env `dvbook` (pyslang 11.0.0; no `slang` binary, no `z3`
or `cvc5` on PATH). Everything written for this review is outside the
repository in `scratchpad/ch05-review/{src,sv,pdf}`. Chapter sources
were stable throughout: `.qmd` MD5 `5a61084b…`, `refs.bib`
`cf2454ee…`. The PDF inspected is `_book/Design-Verification.pdf`,
CreationDate 2026-09-11 20:26:45, 264 pages; Chapter 5 occupies physical
pages 148–174 (printed folios 144–170). `examples/status.json` was copied

## Verdict

**Needs another pass. Five blocking findings.**

The code is sound: all nine examples lint and run, all nine `run.out`
files and `matrix.md` are byte-identical to a fresh run, and every one of
the eleven technical claims the brief listed reproduced on both tools
exactly as the prose states it (§1 below). The rendered table sits
cleanly on two pages and the two-tool reset output is whole on one page.

The chapter's problems are in its attribution and in the table's
coverage of what the chapter teaches. Three sentences attribute to a
"gotchas" paper a claim that paper does not make — one of them cites the
2007 paper for a section that exists only in the 2006 paper, one
describes a scenario that appears in neither, and one attributes to the
2013 synthesis paper a reason it does not give while citing, as
supporting evidence, a paper whose actual recommendation is the opposite
of the chapter's rule. One sentence says the support table records a
reason for a design choice when the table records the reverse. And the
table's "static properties and methods" row says Icarus 13.0 *runs* the
construct, while the chapter's own static-member listing fails to compile
on Icarus at exactly the line the prose singles out, because the probe
was written to avoid the operator the listing uses.

Seven serious and sixteen minor findings follow. None of the serious ones
is a measurement error; four are wording that outruns the source, two are
counts in the prose that disagree with the table, one is a tool behavior
the chapter's exercise depends on and the default simulator does not
exhibit.

## Blocking

**B1 — `:25` attributes to the 2013 synthesis paper a reason it does not
give, and cites as "the second half of the reason" a paper that
recommends the opposite of the chapter's rule.**

`chapters/ch05-testbench-language.qmd:25`:

> in the same paper they recommend declaring random stimulus as `bit`,
> because a randomized `logic` can take the value X, and X is not a
> stimulus a design can be asked to handle [@sutherlandmills2013synth].
> The 2007 gotchas paper supplies the second half of the reason:
> `randomize()` on a signed two-state type ranges over the whole signed
> range, so a stimulus variable's type is also its distribution
> [@sutherland2007gotchas]. Put the two together and the testbench rule
> falls out.

The 2013 paper, fetched from `sutherland-hdl.com`, gives the
recommendation with no reason of its own (`synth2013.raw.txt:215–217`):

```
Note that verification code is a little different. In a testbench, randomly generated test values should be
declared as bit (2-state), rather than logic (4-state). See Sutherland [20] for a detailed exploration of
using 2-state and 4-state types in design and verification code.
```

Reference [20] is "I'm Still in Love with My X!" (`:2631`), already in
`refs.bib` as `sutherland2013x`; its §10.1 is where the guideline lives
(`x2013.raw.txt:2647–2656`: "Verification stimulus variables that will
(or might) have randomly generated values are declared as 2-state
types … 2-state data types should only be used for generating random
stimulus values"). "Because a randomized `logic` can take the value X"
appears in neither paper.

The 2007 paper's §5.4, the passage the chapter paraphrases for the signed
range, says the reverse of what the chapter uses it for
(`g2007.raw.txt:1733–1747`):

```
Synopsis: Unconstrained randomization considers all possible 2-state values within a given data
type.
...
How to avoid this gotcha: When the randomized test variables are to be passed to hardware as
stimulus, it is generally best to use signed types such as logic. This ensures that randomized
values will always be positive values.
```

Its synopsis says randomization produces 2-state values — which is also
why a randomized `logic` does not "take the value X" — and its advice for
stimulus is `logic`, a four-state type. The chapter presents the two
papers as halves of one argument for two-state stimulus; one of them
argues for the other side. (The word "signed" in that sentence of the
paper is evidently a slip for "unsigned"; the recommendation of `logic`
is not.)

Fix: cite the guideline to `sutherland2013x` (§10.1), which states it,
and to the 2013 synthesis paper only as the RTL-side half. Drop "because
a randomized `logic` can take the value X". Cite the 2007 paper for the
signed-range fact alone, and either say that its own advice is `logic`
and why the chapter departs from it, or do not build the rule on it.

**B2 — `:89` cites the 2007 paper for the enum hole; the enum hole is
§6.15 of the 2006 paper, and the 2007 paper does not mention
enumerations.**

`:89`:

> The enum hole. `$cast` and `.next()` refuse a value outside the enum's
> set, and `$cast` says so by returning zero. A static cast —
> `state_t'(3)` — forces the value in … [@sutherland2007gotchas].

`grep -n -i enum g2007.raw.txt` returns nothing. The passage is in the
2006 paper (`g2006.raw.txt:2959–3013`): "an enumerated type variable can
have values that are outside of the defined set of values. Out-of-bounds
values can occur in two ways: uninitialized variables and statically cast
values … the SystemVerilog dynamic $cast function can be used. Dynamic
casting performs runtime error checking, and will not assign an
out-of-bounds value … the .next() method will increment an enumerated
variable to the next label in the enumerated list". The Pitfall's content
matches that section; its citation names the wrong paper. The summary
(`:224`) carries no citation, so it is unaffected.

Fix: `[@sutherland2006gotchas]`.

**B3 — `:151` attributes to the 2007 paper a "mailbox passed by value"
bug that the paper does not contain, and counts three lifetime bugs where
the paper has two.**

`:151`:

> Three lifetime bugs from the 2007 catalogue … A watchdog task declared
> in a module and started twice shares one timer between the two starts.
> A mailbox passed by value into a task and then reassigned inside it
> leaves the caller holding the old one. A handle assigned inside an
> `input` argument updates the task's copy of the handle and not the
> caller's [@sutherland2007gotchas].

The first is §3.10 (`g2007.raw.txt:1117–1180`, the `watchdog` task; note
it is declared in a `program`, not a module). The third is §4.4
(`:1440–1470`). The second is not in the paper. §4.4's example is
(`:1456–1461`):

```
function void build_env(Consumer c, Producer p); // c and p are inputs
mailbox mbx;
mbx = new;
c = new(mbx);
p = new(mbx);
```

The mailbox is constructed *inside* the function and handed to two
constructors; nothing passes a mailbox in and reassigns it. What is
passed by value and reassigned is `c` and `p` — which is the chapter's
third item. The second item is a garbled duplicate of the third, and
"three lifetime bugs from the 2007 catalogue" is two. (The 2006 paper's
§2.10, package tasks shared across the design, would be a genuine third
if a third is wanted.)

Fix: drop the mailbox sentence and write "Two lifetime bugs…", or replace
it with 2006 §2.10 and cite both papers.

**B4 — `:164` says the support table records a reason for preferring
modules with clocking blocks over `program` blocks; the table records the
opposite.**

`:164`:

> This book's testbenches are modules with clocking blocks rather than
> programs, for a reason the support table below records — one of the
> two simulators runs one construct and not the other.

The table (`matrix.md`, fresh run):

```
| clocking block | no build — syntax error | runs |
| program block | runs | runs |
```

Both simulators run `program` blocks; only Verilator runs clocking
blocks. The construct the book chose is the one with narrower support;
the construct it declined runs everywhere. Whatever the real reason for
the choice is (the lowRISC DV guide's rule 1, "Do not use the `program`
construct", `dvstyle.md:1035`, would be a sourced one), the table does
not record it, and a reader who checks the table against this sentence
finds the sentence false.

Fix: state the actual reason and cite it, or delete the clause.

**B5 — The table's "static properties and methods" row says Icarus 13.0
*runs* the construct; the chapter's static-member listing does not
compile on Icarus, at the line the prose singles out, because the probe
avoids the operator the listing uses.**

`:132`:

> `made` belongs to the class, not to any object: every object sees the
> same one, and it is reached through the class scope operator,
> `packet::made`, without an object at all.

`examples/ch05-testbench-language/class-handles/tb_handles.sv:31` uses
`packet::made`. On Icarus:

```
$ iverilog -g2012 -o build/kh.vvp k_handles.sv
k_handles.sv:31: syntax error
k_handles.sv:31: error: Malformed statement
```

With `packet::made` changed to `a.made` the same file compiles and prints
all four lines correctly on Icarus. The probe behind the row,
`probes/i13a_static_instance_call.sv`, reads the static through an
instance (`c1.count()`) and never uses `::`; its own comment in the
research note says that was done because `::` fails. So the row measures
"static members reached through an instance", the chapter teaches "static
members reached through the class scope operator", and the row's *runs*
under Icarus would send a reader to run the chapter's listing on Icarus
and get a syntax error. The chapter's own inheritance row was split for
exactly this kind of difference (`:181`); this row was not.

Fix: split the row as the inheritance row is split — "static member,
through an instance" (runs / runs) and "static member, through `cls::`"
(no build / runs) — and have `:132` or the class-handles Makefile say the
listing is Verilator-only for that reason. (The Makefile says only "Runs
on the default simulator.")

## Serious (not blocking)

**S1 — `:181` "the two cells that say *wrong*" — four cells say it; `:229`
"ten on one" — eleven run on exactly one.**

`matrix.md` has four *wrong* cells: 2-state/4-state (Verilator), virtual
method through a base handle (Icarus), randomize with a constraint
(Verilator), solve-before and soft (Verilator). `:183` goes on to discuss
the last two, so the chapter knows they exist; `:181` still says "two".
The summary at `:229` says "thirteen run on both open simulators, ten on
one, and two run and print the wrong answer in silence"; the caption
says 10 on Verilator only *and* 1 on Icarus only, so eleven run on one.
"Two … in silence" is defensible (the constrained-randomize cells print a
warning to stderr), but only if the sentence says which two. Fix: "the
two cells that say *wrong* for a language construct rather than for a
missing solver", and "eleven on one".

**S2 — `:183` cites the Verilator language-limitations page for the SMT
solver; that page does not mention a solver.**

`:183`: "Verilator delegates constraint solving to an external SMT
solver, and with none installed `randomize()` returns without solving
[@verilator-languages]." The page at the entry's URL, fetched and
searched for `solver`, `SMT`, `SAT`, `z3` and `constraint`, has zero hits
for each. The claim is true (measured: `Process::open: execvp(z3)…
Unable to communicate with SAT solver … VERILATOR_SOLVER`, then `ok=0
v=99` with `v` untouched), but its documentary source is the `Changes`
file's 5.026 entry, "Support constrained randomization with external
solvers", and `refs.bib` already holds `verilator-changes`. Fix: cite
`verilator-changes` and, if the runtime message is wanted, quote it as
measured.

**S3 — The lifetime listing's trap is not exhibitable on the simulator
that runs it; on the other simulator the listing corrupts as written.**

`:148`: "The static task's two callers happen to print the right answers
here, and that is the trap: the corruption depends on timing, and a
testbench that has been correct for a year can become wrong when a delay
changes." Exercise 5 (`:238`) asks the reader to "change the delays until
the two callers print the wrong sums." The listing runs on Verilator
only (its Makefile says "Runs on the default simulator"), because Icarus
rejects `ref`:

```
l_ref.sv:2: sorry: Reference ports not supported yet.
```

On Verilator 5.030 no timing I tried corrupts a static module task —
neither the listing's simultaneous start, nor a one-unit stagger of the
listing's form (`l_static.sv`: `static: 11 21`), nor the 2007 paper's
own argument-overwrite form (`l_static2.sv`: `t=20000 irq=0 cycles=20`,
the first caller keeps its arguments). Icarus corrupts in every form,
including the listing's own unchanged timing with the `ref` task removed:

```
$ vvp build/kl.vvp          # tb_lifetime.sv minus `bump`, Icarus 13.0
static task, two callers: 12 11  (expected 11 21)
automatic task, two callers: 11 21  (expected 11 21)
$ ./build/v_kl/Vtop         # same file, Verilator 5.030
static task, two callers: 11 21  (expected 11 21)
```

So "happen to print the right answers here" is right about the page, but
the promised corruption cannot be produced by the reader on the tool the
book runs the listing on, and the exercise cannot be done there. Fix:
split the example — the static/automatic half has no `ref` and can run
under both tools through a `run_both.sh` like the reset example's, which
would show Icarus's `12 11` beside Verilator's `11 21` and make the point
better than the current text; keep `bump` in a Verilator-only file. Then
say in `:148` which simulator shows the trap, and point exercise 5 at it.

**S5 — `:13` and `:32` present the 2006 paper's four two-state gotchas
as "four ways a two-state testbench hides a bug" and misdescribe the
second.**

`:13`: "Sutherland and Mills catalogued this as the first of four ways a
two-state testbench hides a bug". `:32`: "A state machine whose enum's
first value is the reset state cannot be told apart from one that was
never reset … In each case the four-state version of the same testbench
would have printed an unknown."

The paper's §3.1 is a testbench gotcha (`chip_tb`). §3.2–3.4 are design
models: a `controller` module's enum state machine, a `comparator`
module with unconnected inputs, a `RAM` module read out of bounds
(`g2006.raw.txt`, §3 in full). §3.2's mechanism is lock-up, not
indistinguishability: "When the always_ff state sequencer is reset, it
will assign State the value of WAITE, which is the same value as the
current value of State, and thus does not cause a transition on State.
Since State does not change, the always @(State) combinational
procedural block does not trigger … The simulation is stuck in the
start-up state no matter how many clock cycles are run". And the
four-state version does not "print an unknown"; it triggers the
combinational block ("When State is assigned WAITE during reset, the
always @(State) will trigger"). Fix: "four ways a two-state model hides
a bug, one of them in the testbench"; describe §3.2 as the lock-up it is;
end the Pitfall with what the paper says the four-state version does.

**S6 — `:104` attributes to Sutherland and Mills an explanation they do
not give.**

`:104`: "Sutherland and Mills explain why both are right: a design
bundles signals that belong together electrically, and an interface
there hides the port list a synthesis flow needs to see; a testbench
bundles signals that belong together *procedurally* — everything one
driver touches — and an interface there is the only unit that can be
handed to a class [@sutherlandmills2013synth]."

The paper's §8 says (`synth2013.raw.txt:1829–1833`): "The primary purpose
of interfaces is to bundle together signals that are related in some
way, such as all the signals that make up an AMBA bus … interfaces are
not intended to bundle all the ports of a module together into a single
port. (Verification is a different story — interfaces are often used
between the testbench and the design under test, where the interface
might bundle signals together that are not functionally related.)" It
says nothing about hiding a port list from synthesis (its port-list
remark, `:1704`, is about post-synthesis port naming) and nothing about
classes. "Electrically", "procedurally" and "the only unit that can be
handed to a class" are the chapter's, and good; they should not be
introduced with "Sutherland and Mills explain". Fix: attribute the
related-versus-all-ports distinction and the verification aside to the
paper, and let the rest stand as the chapter's own reasoning.

**S7 — The structs-enums listing fails on Icarus for a reason its

reader.**

`structs-enums/Makefile:1–2`: "`$cast` on an enum is unsupported on

## 6. Clarity, and the line with the next chapter

For a first-year engineer the chapter reads well: each section opens
with a rule in one sentence, shows one listing, and reads the output
back. The reset story is the right way in, and the table is the right
way out. Three places will confuse that reader: the program-block
sentence (**B4**), which says the table argues for a choice it argues
against; the lifetime section (**S3**), which promises a trap the reader
cannot spring on the tool they are told to use; and the gap between
"every listing in this chapter runs" and the four listings that run on
one tool for reasons the table does not show (M16). The type-rule
paragraph (**B1**) is the one place where the reasoning is wrong rather
than merely unsourced: a reader who takes away "a randomized `logic` can
be X" has learned something false.

The line with the next chapter holds. Inheritance appears only as two
table rows and the tool finding at `:181`; no listing uses `extends`,
`virtual` or `super`; `$cast` is shown on an enum only. `:136` draws the
line explicitly and says why. One small leak: `:181` explains virtual
dispatch through a base handle in enough detail that the reader has met
the idea before the chapter that teaches it — acceptable, since it is
stated as a tool fact and deferred in the same sentence.
