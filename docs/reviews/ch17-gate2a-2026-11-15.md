## Blocking findings

### B1. Line 434: the bounded-model-checking sentence says "within $k$ steps"; the formula it introduces says "at step $k$"

Line 434: "A trace of length $k$ is a sequence of states $s_0, \dots, s_k$
... and the property fails within $k$ steps exactly when some such trace
ends in a bad state." The formula of line 436 has `Bad` on $s_k$ only, so
it is satisfiable exactly when a violation is reachable in *exactly* $k$
transitions. "Within $k$ steps" is the disjunction over $i \le k$ (which is
how the cited paper states its translation, with the property checked at
every position up to the bound). The chapter itself has the right reading
later: the definition at line 443 ("asks whether frame $k$ can be bad") and
line 497 ("no trace of exactly this length ends in a violation"). So the
sentence that introduces the central formula of the worked example
contradicts the definition that follows it, and a reader who cites line 434
has a false statement: in a design that passes through a bad state and
leaves it, the property "fails within $k$ steps" for every later $k$ while
the formula of line 436 is unsatisfiable at those bounds.

Fix (one clause): "... and the property fails *at step* $k$ exactly when
some such trace ends in a bad state; it fails within $k$ steps when that
holds for some bound from 0 to $k$, which is why the unroller asks one
bound after another."

Related, non-blocking, same paragraph: the formula writes $T(s_i, s_{i+1})$
and $\mathit{Bad}(s_k)$ without the inputs the prose and the definition
give each frame, and without the constraints the worked example asserts in
every frame (N15).

### B2. Line 224: the Beame, Kautz and Sabharwal result is stated without its qualifier

Line 224: "Beame, Kautz and Sabharwal made the correspondence between
clause learning and resolution precise, showing that clause learning with
unlimited restarts is as strong as general resolution [@beame2004towards]".
The paper's abstract (fetched today through the Crossref record of DOI
10.1613/jair.1410) claims this for a **variant** of clause learning with
unlimited restarts, not for clause learning as solvers implement it. The
result for the unmodified procedure is a later one (Pipatsrisawat and
Darwiche, 2011, listed in the research note section 2.2 and marked there
"[U]: read before stating"). The research note marks the whole statement
`[U]`; the `refs.bib` note says only "Abstract read", and the abstract does
not support the sentence as written.

Also true only under an unstated condition: the equivalence is between
proof systems, that is, with the best possible decisions and restarts. It
says nothing about what a solver with a real heuristic finds, which is the
distinction the chapter draws so carefully forty lines later for
multipliers.

Fix: "... showing that a variant of clause learning with unlimited restarts
is, as a proof system, as strong as general resolution
[@beame2004towards]." The direction the chapter actually uses at line 247
(a clause-learning run that ends in *unsatisfiable* yields a resolution
refutation, so a resolution lower bound binds the solver) needs no
citation beyond the argument the paragraph already gives, and is correct.

### B3. Lines 247, 264, 282, 518, 526 and exercise 12(a): the Haken theorem is claimed from a paper the project records as unread

Line 247: "Armin Haken proved in 1985 that any refutation of the pigeonhole
formulas by resolution has a length exponential in the number of holes
[@haken1985intractability]." The `refs.bib` note for that key reads "text
not consulted"; research note 2.3 says "the paper was **not** read" and
gives the statement from a later paper's description; and `STATUS.md`'s
drafting entry says "Haken 1985 and Burch et al. unread, so nothing is
claimed from them". The chapter claims a theorem from it five times and
builds the theorem/observation distinction of the section, a summary
bullet, a row of @tbl-ch17-engines and exercise 12(a) on it.

The claim is, to my knowledge, correct. What can be confirmed without the
paper: the publisher abstract (read today on a bibliographic mirror of DOI
10.1016/0304-3975(85)90144-6) says the shortest resolution proof of these
formulas "cannot be bounded by any polynomial" of their length, that the
formulas are the pigeonhole tautologies of Cook and Reckhow, and that
extended resolution has polynomial proofs of them. That supports
"superpolynomial", not "exponential".

Fix, either of: (a) read the theorem in the paper and change the bib note
to say which statement was read; or (b) cite the abstract for "no
polynomial bound" and a read source for "exponential" (the Buss and Pitassi
paper the research note already quotes, added to `refs.bib` and cited as
the source of the exponential form). Then correct the `STATUS.md` line.
While there, the abstract's last sentence is worth one clause in the text:
the bound is for resolution, and a stronger proof system has short proofs
of the same formulas (see N9).

### B4. Lines 413, 521 and 529: what z3 does when no rewrite applies is asserted, not measured and not sourced

Line 413: "When no law applies, a bit-vector formula is decided on its
bits". Line 521 (summary): "Bit-vector SMT decides by rewriting where an
algebraic law applies and by bit-blasting where none does." These are
statements about a tool's internals. The example measures two things: that
`simplify` rewrites `a*b != b*a` to `False`, and that the shift-and-add form
takes time growing about fivefold per bit. It does not show that z3
bit-blasts the second form, and no citation is given. Research note 4.1(8)
lists exactly this under "must not be claimed": "Do not describe z3's
internals beyond what the measurement shows". House rule: every claim about
a tool has a source.

Fix, either of: (a) cite a read source for z3's bit-vector procedure and
say "z3"; or (b) keep to the measurement: line 413 "When no law applies,
the cost grew as the bit-level miter's did (@tbl-ch17-mul), which is what
bit-blasting, the definition above, would predict"; line 521 "On the one
family measured, z3 answered at once where a rewrite applied and showed
bit-level growth where none did." The definition of bit-blasting at line
394 is correct and can stay as a definition.

## Non-blocking findings

### Mathematics and precision

**N1. Lines 33, 219, 282: "at most a hundred and fifty lines of Python
each", "these hundred and fifty lines", "a 150-line solver".**
`cdcl/cdcl.py` is 169 lines as a file and 137 lines of code (no blanks,
comments or docstrings); `dpll.py` is 107 and 84. The claim holds only for
code lines. Say "lines of code", or "under 170 lines". (Research note P14
warned of exactly this.)

**N2. Line 27: "The formula for the adders is the larger one."** The adder
side is a CNF of 5,741 clauses (@tbl-ch17-adder). The multiplier side is
the word-level formula of @tbl-ch17-word, whose bit-level size no output
prints. By the pattern of @tbl-ch17-mul (differences 151, 191, 231, 271,
311, 351) the chapter's own 11-bit miter would have 2,123 clauses, so the
sentence is true of the chapter's encodings; it is not shown for whatever
z3 builds. Add "as this chapter encodes them" or print the 11-bit size.

**N3. Line 91, definition of the Tseitin encoding: "at most a constant
number of clauses for each gate".** True for gates of bounded fan-in. The
chapter's own encoder has `or_all`, an $n$-input OR with $n + 1$ clauses,
and every miter uses it. The linear bound is in the number of gate
*inputs* (wires), not gates. Suggested: "a number of clauses proportional
to the gate's fan-in, three or four for the two-input gates of this
chapter".

**N4. Line 155: "a literal's level is the number of decisions made before
it was assigned".** For a decision literal that is off by one: the second
decision is at level 2 (the code sets the level after pushing the
decision, and line 169 says "Its decision at this level was $b_1 = 1$" at
level 2). Suggested: "the number of decisions on the trail when it was
assigned, the decision itself included".

**N5. Line 160: "A clause can become unit only when its second-to-last
unassigned literal becomes false".** Should be "second-to-last literal
that is not false": a clause with a true literal has unassigned literals
that can go false without making it unit, and the watch invariant in the
same sentence is correctly stated in terms of "not false".

**N6. Lines 169 to 173.** (i) "eleven more literals followed ... The sixth
is `37`": 37 is the sixth entry of the printed trail counting the
decision, the fifth propagated literal. Say "the sixth line". (ii) Line 171
and the definition at 176 say the graph has an edge from every other
literal of a reason; the figure draws the lower-level antecedents only to
the right of the cut (variables 3, 13, 27) and omits those of the left
side (2, 8, 20, 35, 38, 39 appear in the printed reasons). The caption
should say "antecedents from earlier levels are drawn only where they
enter the learned clause".

**N7. Lines 188 and 219: what a learned clause is.** "A learned clause is a
fact about the design" generalizes from one checked case. By the
definition at line 185 a learned clause is implied by the *formula*, which
includes the asserted miter output; many learned clauses are true only
under the assumption that a difference exists. This one happens to follow
from the two circuits alone, and the example checks it; say "this learned
clause". Line 219: "*conflicts* ... is the count of lemmas the proof
needed" should be "the solver derived": a checker that trims a proof
routinely discards most of them.

**N8. Lines 247 and 264: the scope of the pigeonhole conclusion.** "No
decision heuristic, no restart policy and no amount of engineering inside
that plan" is right because of "inside that plan". Line 264 drops the
guard: "no SAT solver will ever do pigeonhole well by search alone".
Solvers leave the resolution plan in practice (preprocessing that adds
variables, cardinality detection, symmetry handling), and the abstract of
the cited paper itself notes that extended resolution has polynomial
proofs of these formulas. Suggested: "no solver whose reasoning stays
within resolution will ever do pigeonhole well".

**N9. Lines 249, 282 and the table at 526: "counting arguments
(theorem)".** The theorem is about the pigeonhole formulas, not about
"counting arguments" in designs. The three hardware examples of line 249
(FIFO occupancy, one-hot grant, credit counter) are properties of binary
counters, and the chapter's own worked example proves a statement about a
FIFO's occupancy at sixteen bounds in at most 185 conflicts each. The
analogy is fair as an analogy; the table cell should read "pigeonhole
formulas (theorem); counting in general (by analogy)". Also line 249, "as
@sec-ch09-example did": that helper invariant was introduced there for
another reason (induction starting from unreachable states, where `count`
disagrees with the pointers), not because a solver cannot count. Say
"for a related reason" or drop the pointer.

**N10. Lines 253 to 264: which multiplier.** (i) `gen.multiply` keeps the
low $N$ bits of the product; the prose says "two $N$-bit multipliers" and
never says the product is truncated, although the BDD section's "bit
$N - 1$ of an $N$-bit product" depends on it. One clause. (ii) "It is
*not* a theorem that these formulas are hard for resolution" is true
(there is no such theorem). "Beame and Liew showed the opposite" is true of
the designs their abstract names (array, diagonal and Booth multipliers
polynomial, Wallace trees quasi-polynomial). The chapter's multiplier is a
truncated shift-and-add with ripple-carry rows, which is not literally one
of those. Add "for multipliers of the kinds they treat, close relatives of
this one" so that "Short proofs exist" is not read as proved for the
measured family.

**N11. Line 211.** (i) "the heuristic moves the search to the carry chain,
where the lemmas are short" is an explanation nothing in the example
measures (no learned-clause lengths, no decision histogram). Mark it as
the likely reason or print one supporting number. (ii) The table shows the
heuristic *worse* than the fixed order at every width up to six bits (13
against 12, 35/27, 84/54, 116/106, 229/208) and better from seven. The
prose reports only the win; the multiplier paragraph later makes much of
the same effect. One clause here would prepare it.

**N12. Line 295 against `bdd/robdd.py` lines 61 to 67.** "The number of
satisfying assignments falls out of one walk over the graph" describes a
memoized count. The listed `count` has no table, so it walks paths, not
nodes: fine at these sizes, exponential on a shared diagram. Either add
the table or say "one walk (this small version repeats shared nodes)".

**N13. Line 303.** (i) "the equivalence that cost `dpll.py` 255 decisions
at four bits" was the equivalence of two whole adders (five outputs); the
BDD example compares the carry out only. Say "the carry's part of the
equivalence". (ii) "72 of the 256, which is a second, independent use of
the same number": node 72 is an index into a table and 72 is a count of
inputs; that they are equal is a coincidence and the sentence reads as if
it meant something. Say "by coincidence". (The count itself is right: with
the wrong gate in the top column the carries differ when exactly one of
$a_3$, $b_3$ is set and there is no carry in, $2 \times 36 = 72$.)

**N14. Line 316: "$3N - 1$ decision nodes: three per column".** Three for
every column but the first, which has two (no carry in): $2 + 3(N - 1)$.

**N15. Lines 434 to 438: the formula omits inputs and constraints.** The
prose says $T$ relates "a state and an input" to the next state and the
definition copies the inputs per frame, but the formula is written over
states only, and the constraint literal that the unroller asserts in every
frame (and @fig-ch17-unroll draws) has no term. Either write
$T(s_i, x_i, s_{i+1})$ and $C(s_i, x_i)$, or say that inputs are folded
into the state for the formula.

**N16. Line 397: "That is the arithmetic of a SystemVerilog
`logic [m-1:0]` when no bit is X".** True only when the expression is
evaluated at width $m$. SystemVerilog sizes an expression from its
context, so `a + b` assigned to a wider target keeps the carry that
`bvadd` drops. This is the commonest way a hand-written bit-vector model
disagrees with RTL and belongs in the pitfall at line 423 as a fourth
item.

**N17. Line 413: "its time multiplies by five or more for each bit".**
From @tbl-ch17-word: 6.25/0.82 = 7.6, 31.07/6.25 = 4.97. "By about five or
more".

**N18. Line 477: the 115 variables of bound 0 are not accounted for.**
"Each frame adds 105 variables" explains the step, not the start: bound 0
has 115 = 105 + 1 (the constant, CNF variable 1 in the listing) + 9 (the
uninitialized bits of `count` and `occ`, fresh in frame 0). One sentence;
it is a good check of the latch rule the paragraph before states.

**N19. Line 376.** "Lines 6 to 11 are the next-state logic, one operator
to a line": lines 6 and 9 are constants. Lines 12 to 15, 17 and 18 are not
described, and they are where the assertion becomes `bad` (the `ulte`
against 5, its negation, an AND with the constant 1); a reader looking for
"the property" finds line 19 pointing at line 18 and no account of it.

  ("regenerate its model"), and `bmc_aag.py` stops at bound 20 unless given
  a larger second argument.
- *Exercise 3 is determinate in its first half and not in its second.*
  The 8-bit miter takes $4^8 - 1 = 65{,}535$ decisions (confirmed; 16
  seconds). "Change `gen.py` so that the operand bits are numbered last"
  does not fix a numbering. Rotating the numbers so that gates keep their

  reversing the whole numbering also puts the operands last and gives

  three and six, which the reader will not expect from line 149 ("will
  measure something else") and which "explain the difference" does not
  help with: the explanation (a decision on a gate output does not fix
  the gate's inputs, so the tree is over wires that under-determine the
  operands and is deeper before propagation can evaluate anything) is
  harder than anything the section taught. Fix: state the renumbering
  exactly, say that the count goes up, and ask why. Line 149 also says
  "with the outputs numbered first", which is a third numbering; make the
