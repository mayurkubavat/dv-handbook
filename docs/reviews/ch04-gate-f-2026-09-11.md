## F1 detail — a paragraph about four examples, printed after the fifth

`chapters/ch04-simulation-fundamentals.qmd:189`, in full:

> This chapter's own examples make the point against themselves, and not in the
> way you might expect. None of them declares a timescale in its source. The
> event-driven simulator therefore falls back to its default, which is why the
> regions output above reports finishing "at 6 (1s)" rather than in
> nanoseconds. The other falls back too, to one picosecond, and the only reason
> it reports nanoseconds here is that the build passes it a timescale on the
> command line — a reason no reader of the source could see. Two tools, two
> silent defaults twelve orders of magnitude apart, and one of the answers
> hidden in a build file. The standard permits exactly this: where no timescale
> has been specified, a compiler may or may not apply a default time unit,
> which is why the same design can behave differently on two tools for a
> reason nothing in it records [@sutherland2007gotchas]. Nothing in these
> examples depends on it, which is exactly why nobody noticed.

What the examples actually declare:

```
$ grep -rn '`timescale' examples/ch04-simulation/*/*.sv
examples/ch04-simulation/timescale/tb_timescale.sv:4:`timescale 1ns/1ps
examples/ch04-simulation/timescale/fine.sv:4:`timescale 1ns/1ps
examples/ch04-simulation/timescale/coarse.sv:8:`timescale 1ns/1ns
```

What the builds pass. The four older examples do pass `--timescale 1ns/1ps` to
every Verilator invocation (`race/run_both.sh:16`, `race/lint_both.sh:13`,
`fourstate/run.sh:18,24`, `fourstate/Makefile:7`, `regions/Makefile:13`,
`sampling/Makefile:9`). The new one does not:

```
$ sed -n '19,21p' examples/ch04-simulation/timescale/run.sh
verilator --binary --timing -Wno-DECLFILENAME -Wno-STMTDLY \
    --top-module tb_timescale -Mdir build/v \
    coarse.sv fine.sv tb_timescale.sv >/dev/null
```

And what the reader sees: PDF p. 126 prints `coarse.sv` with `` `timescale
1ns/1ns `` on its eighth line and `fine.sv` with `` `timescale 1ns/1ps `` on
its fourth; p. 127 then says none of the chapter's examples declares one.

The rest of the paragraph is right, and was verified for this review rather
than taken from round E:

```
$ cat f1_default.sv          # no `timescale anywhere, no flags
module f1_default;
  initial begin $printtimescale; #6 $display("f1: after #6, %%t prints %0t", $time); $finish; end
endmodule
$ iverilog -g2012 -o f1.vvp f1_default.sv && vvp f1.vvp
Time scale of (f1_default) is 1s / 1s
f1: after #6, %t prints 6
f1_default.sv:5: $finish called at 6 (1s)
$ verilator --binary --timing -Wno-DECLFILENAME --top-module f1_default -Mdir vf1 f1_default.sv; ./vf1/Vf1_default
Time scale of f1_default is 1ps / 1ps
f1: after #6, %t prints 6
- Verilator: $finish at 6ps; ...
```

Verilator's build produced **zero lines on stderr** — the 1 ps default is
silent, as the chapter says. One second and one picosecond are twelve orders
apart, as the chapter says. The shipped `tb_regions.sv`, run under Verilator
without the flag, finishes "at 10ps"; with `--timescale 1ns/1ps`, "at 10ns".
The source clause is exact: `g2007.txt:1829–1830`, "if a file is read in when
no ‘timescale has been specified at all, then a compiler might, or might not,
apply a default time unit" (see F-S2 on its wording).

One more clause is loose even for the four: "the only reason it reports
nanoseconds here". Nothing the statically scheduled tool prints in this chapter
carries a unit — `race/run_both.sh` and `fourstate/run.sh` filter its output to
`design=`/`count=`/`bare`/`if/else` lines, so its "$finish at …ns" line never
reaches the page. "Reports nanoseconds" describes a line the reader cannot see.

*Fix.* Scope the paragraph to the examples it is about, and drop the clause
about what the tool "reports":

> The other four examples in this chapter make the point against themselves,
> and not in the way you might expect. None of them declares a timescale in its
> source. The event-driven simulator therefore falls back to its default, which
> is why the regions output above reports finishing "at 6 (1s)" rather than in
> nanoseconds. The other falls back too, to one picosecond, and the only reason
> it runs in nanoseconds is that their builds pass it a timescale on the
> command line — a reason no reader of the source could see. […] Nothing in
> those four depends on it, which is exactly why nobody noticed; the example
> above is the one where everything does.

Then re-read the printed page, because that is what this round's E1 fix did not
do for this paragraph.

## Serious (not blocking)

**F-S1 — `:185` says "1 ns" and "1.4 ns"; the listing prints `1000` and
`1.400000`, in two units it never names.**

`:185`:

> The event-driven tool rounds the `#1.4` to 1 ns; the statically scheduled one
> leaves it at 1.4 ns, while its own `$time` still reports 1 ns on the same
> event. The consequence is not a rounding detail. The `fine` module's print
> falls between the two answers…

`examples/ch04-simulation/timescale/run.out`, printed at PDF pp. 126–127:

```
coarse: after #1.4, $time=1000 $realtime=1.000000
fine:   after #1.2, $realtime=1200
…
fine:   after #1.2, $realtime=1200
coarse: after #1.4, $time=1000 $realtime=1.400000
```

The claims are **true** — re-derived here with `$time` printed as an integer
rather than through `%t`:

```
$ cat f3_coarse.sv      # `timescale 1ns/1ns; top declares 1ns/1ps
    #1.4 $display("f3 coarse #1.4: $time=%0d  $realtime=%0f  %%t of $time=%0t  %%t of $realtime=%0t", ...);
$ vvp f3.vvp | grep f3                 # event-driven
f3 coarse #1.4: $time=1  $realtime=1.000000  %t of $time=1000  %t of $realtime=1000
f3 coarse #0.4: $time=3  $realtime=3.000000
$ ./vf3/Vf3_top | grep f3              # statically scheduled
f3 coarse #1.4: $time=1  $realtime=1.400000  %t of $time=1000  %t of $realtime=1400
f3 coarse #0.4: $time=3  $realtime=3.400000
```

`$time` **is** 1 on both tools, `$realtime` is 1.0 on one and 1.4 on the other,
and Icarus rounds even `#0.4` to nothing where Verilator honors it. But the
shipped `coarse.sv:11,13` formats `$time` with `%0t` and `$realtime` with
`%0f`, and `fine.sv:6` formats `$realtime` with `%0t`. `%t` prints in the
design's global precision, which is picoseconds because `fine.sv` declared it;
`%f` prints in the module's own unit, nanoseconds. So one line carries
`$time=1000` beside `$realtime=1.400000` **for the same instant**, the next
carries `1200`, and the prose asks the reader to see that 1200 "falls between"
1.000000 and 1.400000. Nothing on the page says which number is in which unit,
and the chapter has not introduced `%t`. A reader who takes the digits at face
value reads `$time=1000` as "a thousand" and the paragraph's "its own `$time`
still reports 1 ns" as contradicted by the listing above it.

The brief for this round asked whether "the prose claims exactly what it prints
and no more". It claims exactly what the simulators *compute*; the listing
prints it in a form that needs a decoder the chapter does not supply.

*Fix, in the example rather than the prose.* Print every time in the module's
own unit so the four numbers the argument turns on read `1`, `1.000000`,
`1.200000`, `1.400000`:

```systemverilog
    #1.4 $display("coarse: after #1.4, $time=%0d $realtime=%0f",
                  $time, $realtime);
```

and in `fine.sv`, `$realtime=%0f`. Then re-run `scripts/check-examples.sh ch04`
so `run.out` follows. Alternatively keep `%t` and add one sentence: "`%t`
prints in the design's finest precision, so `1000` here is 1 ns."

**F-S2 — `:189` restates a source sentence clause for clause.**

`g2007.txt:1829–1830`:

> if a file is read in when no ‘timescale has been specified at all, then a
> compiler **might, or might not, apply a default time unit**. This can cause
> radically different simulation results when simulating the same design on
> different simulators.

`:189`:

> where no timescale has been specified, a compiler **may or may not apply a
> default time unit**, which is why the same design can behave differently on
> two tools for a reason nothing in it records [@sutherland2007gotchas].

Two consecutive source clauses in the source's order with substituted words
("if a file is read in when" → "where", "might, or might not" → "may or may
not", "radically different simulation results … on different simulators" →
"behave differently on two tools"). Same class as round E's E-S3, which was
closed by quoting; this one is the sentence round E's E-M2 asked the chapter to
cite, and it was cited by paraphrase rather than from understanding. The
citation is on the line, so it is not an attribution defect.

*Fix.* Quote the seven words that carry it — they are Sutherland's own
characterization, which the research note explicitly says to cite as his —
and say the rest from the reader's side:

> The standard, as Sutherland reads it, lets a compiler "might, or might not,
> apply a default time unit" when none has been declared
> [@sutherland2007gotchas]; each of this book's tools took one of those
> options, and nothing in the source records which.

### 2.1 Control flow on an unknown (`:145–147`) — holds on the four-state tool

```systemverilog
// f5_x.sv (excerpt) -- xc = 1'bx, xsel = 2'bxx, all targets pre-set to 7
if (xc) bare = 1;
if (xc) els = 1; else els = 2;
case (xsel) 2'b00: csn = 1; 2'b01: csn = 2; endcase
case (xsel) 2'b00: csd = 1; default: csd = 9; endcase
```

```
$ iverilog -g2012 -o f5.vvp f5_x.sv && vvp f5.vvp | grep f5
f5 bare_if=7 if_else=2 case_nodefault=7 case_default=9
f5 x&1=x x&0=0 x|1=1 x+1=x x==1=x
```

Bare `if` keeps the old value; `if/else` takes `else`; `case` without
`default` keeps the old value; with `default` takes it; `x&0=0` and `x|1=1`
justify the hedge "generally propagate". A two-state tool cannot run this
experiment at all, which is the chapter's own point at `:162`. The source
covers both statement kinds: `x2013.txt:193–199` (§3.1, "should the control
condition evaluate to unknown, the else branch is executed") and `:287–326`
(§3.2, "Case statements without a default-X assignment", Table 2).
