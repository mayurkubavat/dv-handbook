## S-2 (`appendices/appI-agent-tool-layer.qmd:207`) — the sentence names `description` as the place for a limit, then puts half its own example in `$comment`

**The text**, printed with the schema it describes on PDF pages 199-202:

> And the schema records what the tool *cannot* decide in `description`, the
> keyword a consumer is meant to read rather than a note between schema authors:
> this one says in its own summary that no verdict it reports is a sign-off, and
> in its author's note that it reports a recognized shape rather than a judgment
> about safety.

The sentence draws a distinction — `description` is for consumers, `$comment` is
a note between schema authors — and then states, in the same breath, that the
schema's second limit lives in the author's note. Both halves are accurate about
the file; together they contradict the rule the first clause states. The reader's
eye is a page above the spliced source, where it is visible:

```
$ sed -n '5p;102,103p' tools/dvh/schema/crossings.schema.json
  "description": "Crossings between domains. No verdict here is a sign-off.",
  },
  "$comment": "Reports a recognized shape, never whether a crossing is safe."
```

And the split runs through the whole file, not just the root. The schema makes
three statements about what it cannot decide. One is in `description` — `:5`,
"No verdict here is a sign-off." The other two are in `$comment`: `:96`,
"Absence is not correctness; see shape_recognized", and `:103`, "Reports a
recognized shape, never whether a crossing is safe." (The remaining `$comment`s
at `:53`, `:65`, `:72` and `:80` are definitional precision rather than limits,
and `$comment` is the right home for those.) So two of the three limits are in
the keyword the sentence says they are not in.

This is not a factual error about the world, and neither keyword is wrong to use
— but the appendix is teaching a reader where to put a disclaimer so a consumer
finds it, and the example it prints does the opposite for two of its three
limits. A reader who follows the advice and reads only `description` misses
"Absence is not correctness", which is the most load-bearing caveat in the file.

**Fix, two options.**

1. Prose, one clause. Say what the file does: "…records what the tool *cannot*
   decide twice over: in `description`, the keyword a consumer is meant to read —
   this one's summary says that no verdict it reports is a sign-off — and again
   in `$comment`, for whoever next edits the schema, where it says it reports a
   recognized shape rather than a judgment about safety."
2. Or move the disclaimers, and keep the sentence. Promote `:96`'s "Absence is
   not correctness; see shape_recognized" into the `unrecognized` description
   (there is room: that description is 78 columns and the file's maximum is 79),
   and leave `$comment` for genuinely author-facing notes. Then the sentence's
   rule and the file agree.

Whichever is chosen, `tools/dvh/schema/crossings.schema.json` is spliced only
into this appendix (`grep -rn 'include="tools/' chapters/ appendices/` →
`appI:204` and `appI:211` for the schema files; `ch03` splices `design.py`,
`clocks.py` and `test_ch03.py`), so no other page needs re-checking for this one.

## A note on the pattern, since this is the seventh round

The line of failure the last three rounds traced — the appendix asserting a
property of the tool's *output* while the tests checked a property of its
*shape* — is now closed in substance. The location claim is true of every design
the repository ships, and I established that by running the tool rather than by
reading the test that says so. The code fix for same-named arrays is real, keyed
by module, and holds on a design the repository has never seen.

What remains of the pattern is quantitative rather than structural: the sentence
at `:209` says *every* design and means *twenty-nine of thirty-three*, and the
sentence at `:207` states a rule its own printed example follows two times in
seven. Both are visible to anyone who reads the sentence beside the artifact it
describes, and neither survives a fix longer than a paragraph.

The one class of defect this review could not close by running anything is the
bibliographic one. `S-3`, `M-1` and `M-2` are all the same shape: a personal name
in `refs.bib` that no note in the repository evidences, resolved in earlier
rounds by making two documents agree rather than by opening the source. Three
such names are now in the printed bibliography. A cheap standing guard would be
to require that any `author` field naming a person cite, in the corresponding
note, the file or page the name was read from.
