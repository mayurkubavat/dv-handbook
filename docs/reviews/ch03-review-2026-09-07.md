# Chapter 3 review: Digital Design for Verifiers

Scope: the chapter text, its citations against `refs.bib` and the research
note, the example sources it splices in, and the tool code in `tools/dvh/`
that the chapter makes factual claims about. Tool claims were checked by
running the code, not by reading it alone; the constructed test cases are
described inline so they can be reproduced.

**Verdict: needs another draft pass.** Blocking items are listed in §8.

Counts: 6 unsourced claims, 3 constraint violations, 0 vendor-claim
problems, 6 attribution findings, 8 technical errors, 5 template
deviations, 4 clarity findings. 32 findings total.

## 1. Unsourced claims

Every `[@key]` used in the chapter resolves to an entry in `refs.bib`; there
are no dangling citation keys. The problems below are the opposite kind: a
citation that points at the wrong source, or a sentence that carries no
citation and is not supported by the research note.

### 1.1 The 90% / 99% FIFO figures are attributed to the wrong paper (blocking)

`chapters/ch03-digital-design-for-verifiers.qmd:156`

> most incorrectly implemented FIFO designs still function properly ninety
> percent of the time, and most almost-correct ones function properly more
> than ninety-nine percent of the time [@cummings2008cdc]

The research note attributes this sentence to the *asynchronous FIFO* paper,
not the CDC paper: Part B §2.3, asynchronous-FIFO bullet, quotes it as
"Most incorrectly implemented FIFO designs still function properly 90% of
the time. Most almost-correct FIFO designs function properly 99%+ of the
time" from Cummings, *Simulation and Synthesis Techniques for Asynchronous
FIFO Design*, SNUG San Jose 2002, §1.0. `cummings2002fifo` exists in
`refs.bib:876` and is not cited anywhere in the chapter.

**Fix:** change the citation to `[@cummings2002fifo]`. Add the paper to
Further reading, since it is now a cited source.

### 1.2 "Registers with no reset for good reasons" is the exact claim the note forbids

`chapters/ch03-digital-design-for-verifiers.qmd:121`

> Some have no reset for good reasons: a datapath pipeline stage whose
> output is qualified by a valid bit does not need one, and a storage array
> is not meant to reset at all.

No citation, and the research note flags this precise generalization as
unsourced. Part B §5, row 4 lists the claim "Datapath pipeline stages and
RAM/register arrays legitimately have no reset" and rules on it: "**Not
sourced.** Only the follower-flip-flop case is quoted (Cummings §3.1) plus a
vendor bullet acknowledging reset-less elements exist. Needs a further source
before the chapter asserts the general rule."

**Fix:** replace with the case the evidence does carry — the follower
flip-flop, cited to `[@cummings2003resets]` — and state the array case as
the book's own engineering judgment in the same explicit voice used at line
63 ("this book's reading of the definitions above"). Do not present it as
established practice.

### 1.3 The reset-test In Practice box asserts unmeasured prevalence

`chapters/ch03-digital-design-for-verifiers.qmd:118`

> Every block needs a reset test, and most have one: assert reset, release
> it, check the register values. The two that are usually missing are the
> ones that find bugs.

Three empirical claims — "most have one", "usually missing", and the
implicit claim that these two are the bug-finding ones — with no citation on
any of them. The research note, Part B §5 row 5, rates the family
"Half-sourced": Hunter et al. name the idle / active / soft / multi-domain
test classes and observe that reset testing often lacks self-checking
autonomy, but "nobody fetched here measures how many blocks skip these, and
no source gives a clock-stopped reset test guideline."

`hunter2013resettesting` exists at `refs.bib:909` and is cited nowhere in
the chapter, so the one source that does support half of this box is unused.

**Fix:** cite `[@hunter2013resettesting]` for the test taxonomy (idle,
active/mid-traffic, soft, multi-domain), drop "most have one" and "usually
missing", and derive the clock-stopped case where the note derives it: from
the design side, `[@cummings2003resets]` §4.3, a gated clock may be disabled
coincident with reset assertion, therefore the scenario exists and therefore
it is testable. Phrase it as a recommendation, not as a survey result.

### 1.4 "The practice is unambiguous" overstates what OpenTitan prescribes

`chapters/ch03-digital-design-for-verifiers.qmd:129`

> what can be cited is the engineering practice, and the practice is
> unambiguous.

The research note is explicit that it is not. Part A §2.2, after quoting the
OpenTitan sparse-encoding and Hamming-distance guidance: "Note the limit:
that page prescribes *detection* (alerts, 'clearing/randomizing state, cease
processing') but does not mandate a specific recovery action on invalid
state." The chapter's own preceding sentence is about what happens on an
illegal encoding, so a reader takes "unambiguous" to cover the response, and
it does not.

**Fix:** "what can be cited is the engineering practice: OpenTitan
prescribes detection — sparse encodings, a minimum Hamming distance, and an
alert — and leaves the recovery action to the design."

### 1.5 "The standard open one" is a ranking no source makes

`chapters/ch03-digital-design-for-verifiers.qmd:165`

> pre-built libraries of such checkers exist, of which the Accellera Open
> Verification Library is the standard open one [@accellera2014ovl].

The note (Part C §1.5) supports only that OVL is "a library of assertion
checkers intended to be used by design, integration, and verification
engineers", version 2.8.1, 2014-04-08. "The standard open one" is a claim
about the field.

**Fix:** "of which the Accellera Open Verification Library is an openly
licensed example [@accellera2014ovl]." If the chapter wants to note that OVL
has not moved since 2014, the note carries the date and that is a fair,
sourced observation.

### 1.6 Tool counts are the researcher's, presented as the documentation's

`chapters/ch03-digital-design-for-verifiers.qmd:180`

> Verilator's warning list carries 136 named warnings, of which 18 are off
> by default [...] Verible's published rule list carries 61 rules, 42 on by
> default [@verible-lint-rules].

Both counts are correct as of the note's access date, but neither page
states a total; Part A §3.1 and §3.2 say the numbers were "counted from" the
pages on 2026-09-07. Presented bare, they read as documented figures, and
they are live web pages that will drift.

**Fix:** "as of September 2026, Verilator's warning list carries 136 named
warnings, 18 of them off by default (counted from the list itself)". Same
treatment for the Verible figures.

### 2.1 IEEE 1800-2023 is cited as the source for interface and modport behavior (blocking)

`chapters/ch03-digital-design-for-verifiers.qmd:161`

> An `interface` bundles the signals that cross a boundary into one
> declaration, and a `modport` states the direction each participant sees,
> so that a connection which disagrees with the contract fails at
> elaboration rather than in a waveform [@ieee1800-2023].

This is constraint 1. The standard is paywalled and was not read — the
`refs.bib` entry says so itself: "Paywalled; clause text not consulted."
Using it as the citation for a substantive behavioral claim describes the
contents of a document nobody on this book has opened. Part C §2.1 is
explicit: "The LRM itself is paywalled, so the chapter should cite the
clause by name and number but must not quote LRM text that has not been
read."

Compounding it, "fails at elaboration rather than in a waveform" is not in
the note at all. The only sourced statement about modports is second-hand,
from the Yosys manual: it supports "SystemVerilog interfaces (SVIs),
including modports for specifying whether ports are inputs".

**Fix:** name the standard and the clause without citing it as evidence, and
source the behavioral claim to something that was read:

> An `interface` bundles the signals that cross a boundary into one
> declaration, and a `modport` names the direction each participant sees.
> Both are defined in clause 25 of IEEE 1800; this book quotes no clause
> text, because the standard is not openly readable. Tool support confirms
> the direction contract is machine-readable: Yosys lists modports among the
> constructs it understands, "for specifying whether ports are inputs"
> [@yosys-verilog].

That needs a `yosys-verilog` entry (`Yosys manual: Verilog support`, in the
note's Part C §5 key list as `yosys2026verilog`); it is not in `refs.bib`
yet. Alternatively, drop the elaboration claim and keep only what the
chapter can defend: that the modport is where the direction contract is
written down.

### 2.2 "Not reproducible in simulation at all" is the wording constraint 4 forbids

`chapters/ch03-digital-design-for-verifiers.qmd:23`

> And several of them, the ones involving asynchronous clocks in particular,
> are not reproducible in simulation at all, because the simulator is
> modeling a different circuit from the one that will be built.

Constraint 4 requires the claim to be phrased as "does not model
metastability and diverges from silicon in both directions", never as an
absolute. Litterick's own wording is weaker than the chapter's: the effects
"do not *typically* manifest themselves in standard RTL simulation flows".
"Not reproducible at all" is stronger than any of the five sources, and it
also contradicts the chapter's own careful correction 79 lines later at line
102, which says the honest statement is the two-directional one.

This is the motivating paragraph of the chapter, so the strong version is
what most readers will retain.

**Fix:** "And several of them, the ones involving asynchronous clocks in
particular, do not show up reliably in simulation at all, because the
simulator is modeling a different circuit from the one that will be built —
one that diverges from silicon in both directions, as @sec-ch03-cdc shows."

### 2.3 IEEE 1364.1's contents are described from an unread standard (borderline)

`chapters/ch03-digital-design-for-verifiers.qmd:35`

> The synthesizable subset is fixed by its own standard, IEEE 1364.1,
> separate from the simulation language [@ieee1364.1-2002;
> @cummings2016logicprocs].

Constraint 1 says: "Name the standard (citing Cummings 2016); do not
describe its contents." "Fixes the synthesizable subset" is a statement
about its contents. It happens to be a fair reading of its title, and
Cummings calls it "the RTL synthesis standard", so this is the mildest of
the three — but the `refs.bib` entry for `ieee1364.1-2002` asserts "named
only, contents not described", and the chapter's sentence makes that note
false.

**Fix:** attribute the characterization rather than asserting it: "The
synthesizable subset has a standard of its own, separate from the simulation
language: IEEE 1364.1-2002, which Cummings — who sat on the working group —
calls the RTL synthesis standard [@cummings2016logicprocs]."

## 4. Attribution and originality

The chapter's substantive paraphrasing is mostly good — the metastability
section, the crossing structures and the lint-limits argument are written in
the book's own words. The problem is concentrated in one habit: sentences
introduced with a phrase that announces a quotation ("in their words",
"state it exactly", "the sentence to remember") which then reproduce the
source nearly verbatim, without quotation marks and without the citation on
the same line. AGENTS.md is specific: "Direct quotation is limited to a
sentence or two, in quotation marks, with the citation on the same line."

### 4.1 "Cummings and Mills state it exactly" — near-verbatim, unquoted, uncited on the line

`chapters/ch03-digital-design-for-verifiers.qmd:111`

Chapter:

> Cummings and Mills state it exactly: the problem with asynchronous resets
> is that they are asynchronous both at assertion and at de-assertion, the
> assertion is a non-issue, and the de-assertion is the issue. If reset is
> released at or near an active clock edge, a flip-flop's output can go
> metastable and the reset state of the chip is lost.

Source (research note, Part B §3.1, quoting §5.4):

> "The biggest problem with asynchronous resets is that they are
> asynchronous, both at the assertion and at the de-assertion of the reset.
> The assertion is a non issue, the de-assertion is the issue. If the
> asynchronous reset is released at or near the active clock edge of a
> flip-flop, the output of the flip-flop could go metastable and thus the
> reset state of the ASIC could be lost."

That is word-for-word apart from ASIC → chip and light compression. The
`[@cummings2003resets]` on this paragraph sits two sentences earlier.

**Fix:** make it an explicit quotation and put the citation on the line:

> Cummings and Mills state it exactly: "The assertion is a non issue, the
> de-assertion is the issue. If the asynchronous reset is released at or
> near the active clock edge of a flip-flop, the output of the flip-flop
> could go metastable and thus the reset state of the ASIC could be lost"
> [@cummings2003resets].

Keeping "ASIC" inside the quotation marks is required; the generalization to
"chip" belongs in the book's own following sentence.

### 4.2 "In their words" — same pattern

`chapters/ch03-digital-design-for-verifiers.qmd:33`

Chapter: "In their words, these mismatches are frequently not discovered
until after silicon has been generated, and then require a second spin."

Source: "Frequently, these mismatches are not discovered until after silicon
has been generated, and thus require the design to be re-submitted for a
second spin."

Announced as a quotation, printed as bare prose, and the
`[@millscummings1999mismatch]` is on the previous sentence.

**Fix:** quotation marks around the reproduced clause and
`[@millscummings1999mismatch]` at the end of this sentence, or paraphrase
further from understanding and drop "in their words".

### 4.3 "The sentence to remember" — verbatim without quotation marks

`chapters/ch03-digital-design-for-verifiers.qmd:51`

Chapter: "Cummings' summary is the sentence to remember: these directives
are always most dangerous when they work."

Source: "these directives are always most dangerous when they work!" —
verbatim, exclamation mark aside.

**Fix:** `Cummings' summary is the sentence to remember: "these directives
are always most dangerous when they work!" [@cummings1999fullparallel].`
This is a good line to quote directly; it just has to be marked as one.

### 4.4 The reset-synchronizer guideline is reproduced, and silently widened

`chapters/ch03-digital-design-for-verifiers.qmd:113`

Chapter: "the paper's guideline for it is in capitals: every design using an
asynchronous reset should include one, because without it the reset's
usefulness in the final system is void even if the reset works in

Source: "Guideline: EVERY ASIC USING AN ASYNCHRONOUS RESET SHOULD INCLUDE A

Two problems. The second clause is verbatim and unmarked. And the chapter
says "every design" where the paper says "EVERY ASIC" — the scope is

**Fix:** quote the guideline as the paper states it, in quotation marks with
the citation on the line, and if the chapter wants the wider claim, make it
the book's own sentence after the quotation.

### 4.5 The TileLink block quote silently merges two separate rules

`chapters/ch03-digital-design-for-verifiers.qmd:145`

The quoted block runs together the third and fourth bullets of TileLink
§4.1's rule list as continuous prose. The join is honest in substance — the

Related, minor: `chapters/ch03-digital-design-for-verifiers.qmd:147`, "The

on the line even though it paraphrases the specification's
"Anything not forbidden is allowed" paragraph. Add
`[@sifive2017tilelink]`.

### 4.6 An unnamed engineer is given a motive no source supports

`chapters/ch03-digital-design-for-verifiers.qmd:153`

> That `+1` is the off-by-one, made explicit by an engineer who had met it

## 5. Technical errors

The chapter makes four specific factual claims about `tools/dvh/` and one
about the lint example. Three of the five are wrong. Each was checked by
running the committed code against a purpose-built SystemVerilog file; the
files are reproduced here so the author can re-run them.

### 5.1 The tool does *not* keep a gated clock in its source's domain (blocking)

`chapters/ch03-digital-design-for-verifiers.qmd:227`

> The clock and reset trees are then backward walks from each register's
> clock or reset pin to a source, passing through buffers, gates and
> multiplexers **so that a gated clock is reported with its gating logic
> rather than mistaken for a new domain**, as @sec-ch03-clocks requires.

The code does the opposite. `tools/dvh/clocks.py:71-79`: `_walk_to_sources`
returns *every* source reachable backwards, including the gate's enable
input, and the domain key is then built by joining all of them:

```
roots = sorted({s.name for s in srcs})
...
key = "+".join(info["clock_sources"])
```

So a register behind `clk & en` lands in domain `"clk+en"`, not `"clk"`.
Run on this file:

```systemverilog
module g (input logic clk, rst_n, en, d, output logic q_free, q_gated);
  logic gclk;
  assign gclk = clk & en;
  always_ff @(posedge clk or negedge rst_n)
    if (!rst_n) q_free <= 1'b0; else q_free <= d;
  always_ff @(posedge gclk or negedge rst_n)
    if (!rst_n) q_gated <= 1'b0; else q_gated <= q_free;
endmodule
```

the committed tool reports:

```
DOMAINS: {"clk+en": ["q_gated"], "clk": ["q_free"]}
CROSSINGS (unsynchronized): q_free (clk) -> q_gated (clk+en), 1 bit
```

Two consequences, both bad, and the second is worse than the first. The
gated register is placed in a separate clock domain — exactly the mistake
the sentence claims the tool avoids. And because `crossings()` compares
those domain keys (`tools/dvh/clocks.py:133-140`), an ordinary
same-clock synchronous path across a clock gate is reported as
`BUG ... no synchronizer`, and the tool exits 1. Any real design with clock
gating gets a false failure from a tool the chapter proposes putting in a
regression (line 259).

It is worse with the latch-based glitch-free gate that
`chapters/ch03-digital-design-for-verifiers.qmd:63` recommends, because
`$dlatch` is in neither `DFF_TYPES` nor `PASS_THROUGH`, so the walk falls
through to `tools/dvh/clocks.py:64` and the diagnostic string leaks into
the report as a domain name:

```
DOMAINS: {"clk+en_lat (driven by $dlatch)": ["q_gated"], "clk": ["q_free"]}
```

**Fix, two parts.** In the code: separate the *clock* root from the
*control* inputs. A gate or mux input that is itself a clock (traces back to
a primary clock or a register's Q used as a clock) is a clock source; every
other input is gating control and belongs in `through`, not in
`clock_sources`. For `$mux`/`$pmux` the select is already excluded; the
same treatment is needed for the enable side of `$and`/`$or`. Domain
identity should then be the clock root alone, with the gating logic recorded
in `through` — which is what `gated_or_muxed` already exists to report.

In the chapter: until the code does that, line 227 must not claim the
behavior. If the author prefers to leave the code as-is for now, the honest
sentence is: "The walk records the gating logic it passes through. It does
not yet distinguish a gate's clock input from its enable, so a gated clock
is currently reported as its own domain — a limitation the exercises return
to." That would also need a matching caveat at line 241, since the example
design has no clock gating and therefore never exposes it.

Note that the *design* claim in the Summary
(`chapters/ch03-digital-design-for-verifiers.qmd:268`, "A gated clock keeps
its source's domain; a multiplexed clock may not") is correct and well
reasoned — the problem is only the claim about the tool.

### 5.2 The crossing detector does not follow "every combinational cell" (blocking)

`chapters/ch03-digital-design-for-verifiers.qmd:232`

> The crossing detector walks *data* cones with `any_comb=True`, **following
> every combinational cell** rather than only the clock-tree set, because a
> crossing that arrives through an adder is still a crossing.

An adder works. A mux select does not. `tools/dvh/clocks.py:54` excludes the
`S` port from the walk unconditionally, in both modes:

```python
if cell.dirs.get(port) == "input" and port not in ("S",):
```

So a foreign-domain register that reaches a destination register's `D`
through a multiplexer's *select* is invisible. Run on:

```systemverilog
module muxsel (input logic bclk, brst_n, cclk, crst_n, sel_in,
               input logic [7:0] a, b, output logic [7:0] out);
  logic sel_b;
  always_ff @(posedge bclk or negedge brst_n)
    if (!brst_n) sel_b <= 1'b0; else sel_b <= sel_in;
  always_ff @(posedge cclk or negedge crst_n)
    if (!crst_n) out <= 8'h00; else out <= sel_b ? a : b;
endmodule
```

the tool reports `crossings: []`. That is a genuine unsynchronized
single-bit control crossing — the textbook case — reported as a clean
design.

A second inaccuracy in the same sentence: `combinational` at
`tools/dvh/clocks.py:49-50` is defined as "not a flip-flop and not `$mem*`",
so latches (`$dlatch`, `$adlatch`, `$dlatchsr`, `$sr`) are walked *through*
as though they were combinational. They are state elements. A crossing that
lands on a latch is neither reported as a register nor stopped at.

**Fix, code:** the `S` exclusion is right for the clock walk (a mux select
is not a clock) and wrong for the data walk. Gate it on the mode:

```python
skip_select = (not any_comb) and cell.type in ("$mux", "$pmux", "$_MUX_")
if cell.dirs.get(port) == "input" and not (skip_select and port == "S"):
```

and add the latch cell types to a `LATCH_TYPES` set that the walk stops at.

**Fix, chapter:** "following every combinational cell" must become
"following combinational cells rather than only the clock-tree set", with
the select-path and latch limits stated — this is a chapter about what tools
cannot see, so its own tool's blind spots belong in the text.

### 5.3 `_feeds_directly` does not encode the synchronizer recognition rule

`chapters/ch03-digital-design-for-verifiers.qmd:232`

> And `_feeds_directly` encodes the synchronizer recognition rule from
> @sec-ch03-cdc: a first stage is fed by the foreign register with no logic
> in between, and feeds a second register in its own domain the same way.

`tools/dvh/clocks.py:118-124` is only the first half. `_feeds_directly`
answers exactly one question — does `source` reach this register's `D` with
nothing in between. The rule the sentence describes is assembled in
`crossings()` at `tools/dvh/clocks.py:143-149`, which calls
`_feeds_directly` twice and adds a third condition the chapter omits:

```python
synchronizer = direct and bool(second_stage) and width == 1
```

The `width == 1` term is not incidental — it is the term that makes the
chapter's eight-bit `cfg` crossing fail even if a two-flop structure were
wrapped around it, and it is the code's expression of the "synchronizers
synchronize control, not data" rule from line 91. Dropping it from the
description loses the most important part.

**Fix:** "`_feeds_directly` answers half the question — is this register fed
by the foreign register with nothing in between — and `crossings` assembles
the rest of the rule from @sec-ch03-cdc: a first stage fed directly, feeding
a second register in its own domain the same way, and one bit wide. A
structure missing any of the three is not recognized as a synchronizer."

### 5.4 The tool passes the per-bit-synchronizer anti-pattern in silence (blocking)

`chapters/ch03-digital-design-for-verifiers.qmd:241` and `:243`

> The two unsynchronized crossings on `cfg_b` are the rule being broken, and
> the tool exits non-zero on them, so a build can gate on it. [...] the tool
> decides what *is*, and a person decides what it *means*.

Because the `width == 1` test in §5.3 is applied to the *destination
register's* width, a multi-bit bus crossed through one one-bit synchronizer
per bit is recognized as a set of legal synchronizers. Run on an eight-bit
version of exactly that structure — eight independent two-flop chains, one
per bit of `cfg_b` — the committed tool reports:

```
unsynchronized count: 0
cfg_b -> m0 1 sync= True
```

Zero findings, exit 0, build passes. That is the structure the chapter
quotes Ginosar calling "a complete loss of data" at line 93, and which line
95 says "cannot be guaranteed to have identical latency". The book's own
tool blesses it.

This is a false negative on the chapter's headline rule, and the chapter
never says so. Exercise 5 (line 282) gets close — it asks the reader to try
the four correct structures and decide "whether the tool or the design is
wrong" — but the reader is not told that the *incorrect* structure passes,
and a reader who runs the tool on a per-bit design will conclude it is fine.

**Fix, chapter (required whether or not the code changes):** add the limit
to the worked example, at line 243 where the division of labor is being set
up. Something like: "The recognizer's rule is structural, and structure is
not intent. Eight one-bit synchronizers on the eight bits of a bus match the
shape and are reported as eight correct synchronizers, even though
@sec-ch03-cdc says that structure is a loss of data. A tool that decides
what *is* will approve a shape a person has to reject."

**Fix, code (optional, and the better answer):** after classifying, group
recognized synchronizers by their source register's *net*; if more than one
bit of a single multi-bit source net is synchronized by parallel one-bit
chains into the same destination domain, report the group as a divergence
finding rather than as N synchronizers.

### 5.5 Registers sharing a Q net collapse, so the reported counts are net counts

`tools/dvh/design.py:118-124`, `tools/dvh/clocks.py:74`, `:135`

`registers()` names each flip-flop by the net on bit 0 of its `Q`. When
Yosys emits a bit-blasted register as N one-bit cells on the same net, all N
get the same name, and both `clock_tree`'s `regs[name] = ...` and
`crossings`'s `by_name = {name: cell for ...}` are plain dict assignments —
so N−1 of them are silently dropped. On the per-bit file from §5.4:

```
register yield count: 18
counts: {'m0': 8, 'cfg_c': 8, 'acc': 1, 'cfg_b': 1}
clock_tree register entries: 4
```

Fourteen of eighteen registers vanish. The chapter's report line reads
`cclk: 5 registers` and the chapter repeats it as a register count at
`chapters/ch03-digital-design-for-verifiers.qmd:241` ("with `cclk` holding
five registers"). For the chapter's own design that is true, because every
register there has a distinct net. In general it is a count of distinct Q
nets, not of registers.

**Fix, code:** disambiguate colliding names, e.g. append the bit range
(`m0[3]`) or the cell name when a net already has an entry. The FIFO test at
`tools/dvh/tests/test_ch03.py:64` already depends on Yosys handing back
distinct `mem[i]` names, so the current behavior is load-bearing by
accident.

**Fix, chapter:** if the code is not changed, `:241` should say "five
registers, one per named net" or the report label should change.

### 5.6 "Verilator's strictest lint" is not what the example runs

`chapters/ch03-digital-design-for-verifiers.qmd:211`

> Verilator's strictest lint runs over these four files and reports nothing

`examples/mk/sv.mk:40-41` sets:

```
VERILATOR_FLAGS ?= --timing --timescale $(TIMESCALE) -Wall \
                   -Wno-DECLFILENAME -Wno-UNUSEDSIGNAL
```

Two warning classes are explicitly suppressed, and `sv.mk:38-39` says so in
its own comment: "The -Wno-* switches keep UVM's own source from failing
lint; drop them for plain SystemVerilog examples if you want the strictest
checking." Beyond that, `-Wall` enables the style warnings but not every one
of the 18 disabled-by-default warnings — including `SYNCASYNCNET`, which the
chapter itself names at line 187 as off by default and crossing-adjacent.
So the chapter calls a run "strictest" that is neither the strictest
available nor the one its own Pitfall would want.

**Fix:** "Verilator's `-Wall` lint runs over these four files and reports
nothing." If the author wants the stronger claim, drop
`-Wno-DECLFILENAME -Wno-UNUSEDSIGNAL` for this example (it has no UVM
source) and add the crossing-adjacent defaults-off warnings explicitly,
e.g. `-Wwarn-SYNCASYNCNET`, which would make the "even with the
crossing-adjacent warnings on, nothing" point far stronger than it currently
is.

### 5.7 The lint example's `run.out` misstates the command that produced it

### 5.8 Two smaller code-level issues worth fixing before the chapter ships the file

- `tools/dvh/clocks.py:89` takes `cell.conns[pin][0]` for `$dffsr`/`$dffsre`,

  precisely the case a verifier needs to see.

## 7. Clarity

### 7.1 "Beat" is used before it is defined

`chapters/ch03-digital-design-for-verifiers.qmd:140`

The Definition callout introduces the handshake as "the sender raises
`valid` to offer a beat and the receiver raises `ready` to accept one".
"Beat" is TileLink vocabulary and is the term the whole section turns on —
it recurs at 145, 147, 149 and 151 — but it is never defined. A first-year
DV engineer reading this cold does not know whether a beat is a transaction,
a cycle, or a word.

**Fix:** one clause in the Definition: "a **beat** is one cycle's worth of
payload, the unit the two wires arbitrate over; a message may take several".

### 7.2 The skid buffer is named, then required in an exercise

`chapters/ch03-digital-design-for-verifiers.qmd:151`, exercise 4 at `:281`

The chapter gives the skid buffer one sentence — a stage that registers
`ready` needs two slots, and the structure that provides them is a skid
buffer — and then exercise 4 asks the reader to "decide whether the stage
needs a skid buffer, and say why". The reader has been told the name and the
slot count, but not the mechanism: *why* registering `ready` means the
upstream stage has already launched a beat by the time it learns about
back-pressure. The research note (Part C §1.3) has that argument in one
sentence and the chapter drops it.

**Fix:** add the causal step at 151: "because a registered `ready` reaches
the sender a cycle late, the sender has already launched a beat by the time
it learns to stop, and that beat needs somewhere to go rather than being
dropped [@gisselquist2019skid]."

### 7.3 The reachability citations are given a history they do not have

`chapters/ch03-digital-design-for-verifiers.qmd:127`

> Symbolic reachability analysis is the natural tool for this question and
> long predates its use in this industry [@coudert1990reachability;
> @clarke1981modelchecking].

Coudert, Berthet and Madre 1990 is a hardware paper — verification of
synchronous sequential machines — so it does not predate the technique's use
in this industry; it *is* that use. The sentence as written will confuse a
reader who then opens the citation.

**Fix:** "Symbolic reachability analysis is the natural tool for this
question, and the algorithms are older than most of the tools that ship them
[@coudert1990reachability]; the property-checking frame they sit in is older
still [@clarke1981modelchecking]."

### 7.4 Learning objective 2 promises a step the chapter never teaches

`chapters/ch03-digital-design-for-verifiers.qmd:9`

> classify each crossing between them as safe, **waived**, or a bug

Waivers appear nowhere else in the chapter. The reader is never told what a
waiver is, who grants one, or where it is recorded. The Future Directions
box at 261 comes closest — "nor which crossings are waived" — but that is a
statement that the tool cannot know, addressed to a reader who has not been
told what the thing is.

**Fix:** either define the waiver in one sentence in @sec-ch03-cdc (a
crossing a named person has reviewed and accepted, with the reason recorded
where the next reader will find it — which connects directly to
@sec-ch02) or drop "waived" from the objective. Given that line 176 makes
"everything the RTL assumes about its environment" the output of the
reading, the first option is the better fit.

Minor, not blocking: reset-domain crossing is absent from the chapter
entirely, though the research note has a full subsection on it (Part B
§3.4). The Pitfall at 187 tells the reader that lint does not check
crossings and the worked example then checks clock-domain crossings, which
may leave the impression that reset crossings are covered. One sentence in
@sec-ch03-resets saying that a reset crossing can lie entirely inside a
single clock domain, and so is invisible to a clock-comparison analysis like
the one this chapter builds, would close it — flagged as the book's own
reasoning on a vendor sentence, as the note requires.

## 8. Verdict

**Needs another draft pass.**

Blocking items, by finding number:

- **5.1** — the chapter states that the tool keeps a gated clock in its
  source's domain. It does not; it creates a new domain and then reports a
  false `BUG` crossing across the gate. Either the code or the sentence has
  to change before this ships.
- **5.2** — "following every combinational cell" is false: mux selects are
  excluded and latches are walked through as combinational, so a real
  unsynchronized control crossing through a mux select is reported as a
  clean design.
- **5.4** — the tool passes eight per-bit synchronizers on an eight-bit bus
  with zero findings, which is the structure the chapter itself calls a
  complete loss of data. The limit must be stated in the worked example.
- **2.1** — `[@ieee1800-2023]` is cited as the source for interface and
  modport behavior, from a standard the evidence record says was not read.
  Constraint 1.
- **1.1** — the 90% / 99% FIFO figures are cited to `@cummings2008cdc`; they
  come from `@cummings2002fifo`, which is in `refs.bib` and uncited.
- **1.2** and **1.3** — two passages assert exactly what the research note's
  unsourced-claims tables say must not be asserted without a further source
  (reset-less registers as a general rule; the prevalence of missing reset
  tests).
- **2.2** — "not reproducible in simulation at all" at line 23 is the
  absolute wording constraint 4 forbids, in the chapter's motivating
  paragraph.

Not blocking but should be fixed in the same pass: 1.4, 1.5, 1.6, 4.1–4.6,
5.3, 5.5, 5.6, 5.7, 5.8, and all of §6 and §7.

What is working, and should not be disturbed: the metastability section
(75–85) is the best-sourced writing in the chapter and handles the

flags the gated-versus-muxed distinction as the book's own inference in the

vendor marketing claim appears anywhere in the chapter.
