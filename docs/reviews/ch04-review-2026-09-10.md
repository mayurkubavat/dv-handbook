Scope: the chapter text against the research note's fourteen constraints, every
citation key against `refs.bib` and against the sentence it is attached to, the
four examples under `examples/ch04-simulation/` **run rather than read**, the

## 4. Technical errors

### 4.1 What an unknown does to an `if` is wrong (blocking)

`chapters/ch04-simulation-fundamentals.qmd:138`

> An `if` whose condition is unknown takes neither branch, so the assignments
> inside simply do not happen and the simulation continues with whatever the
> target held before.

Wrong for `if...else`, which is the form that matters: the `else` branch
executes. That is the X-optimism mechanism the sentence is trying to teach, and
the note states it plainly (Part B §4, from Sutherland 2013 §3.1: "If an
`if...else` control condition is unknown, the `else` branch executes"). Verified
under Icarus with a purpose-built case:

```
if/else with unknown cond -> y=2   (2 means the else branch ran)
bare if with unknown cond  -> y=170 (170 means neither branch ran)
case with unknown selector -> y=12  (12 means default was taken)
```

The chapter's own example does not expose the error, because
`examples/ch04-simulation/fourstate/dut_unreset.sv:15` and `:18` are both bare
`if`s with no `else` — so the text is right about the example and wrong about the
language. A verification engineer reading 138 will conclude that an unknown
enable is always inert, and will be surprised the first time an `else` runs on
an unknown condition.

Fix: "an `if...else` whose condition is unknown takes the `else` branch, and a
bare `if` takes neither; either way nothing announces it." The note supplies two
more mechanisms worth a clause each, since they are the same failure: a `case`
with an unknown selector matches nothing and falls to `default`, and
`casex`/`casez` are worse, because the wildcard masks the unknown out and picks
a branch.

### 4.2 The Reactive region is described as holding all testbench code (blocking)

`chapters/ch04-simulation-fundamentals.qmd:56`

| Region | What executes there |
|---|---|
| Reactive | the testbench's own blocking assignments and program code |

Testbench code does not land in Reactive by virtue of being testbench code. The
Reactive region holds the blocking assignments of `program` code, the right-hand
side evaluation of program non-blocking assignments, program continuous
assignments, `$exit`, and the pass/fail action blocks of concurrent assertions
(note Part A §2.1). A testbench written as a module — which is how most of them
are written, and which the note explicitly says is permitted and historically
usual — executes in Active, exactly like the design.

The chapter contradicts itself on this three sections later, and the later
statement is the correct one:

`chapters/ch04-simulation-fundamentals.qmd:106`

> A driver assigning with `=` in a clocked block is in Active, next to the
> design's own reads.

A first-year reader with a module-based testbench takes the table at face value
and concludes their driver is in Reactive and therefore cannot race the design.
That is precisely the belief the chapter opened by demolishing. Fix the table
row to name `program` code and assertion action blocks, and see §7.1.

### 4.3 The isolation claim is over-broad and the mechanism given is not the mechanism (blocking)

`chapters/ch04-simulation-fundamentals.qmd:44`

> That symmetry is why a testbench driving from the Reactive region cannot race
> a design driving from the Active one: they are not in the same part of the
> timestep at all.

Two problems, and they compound §4.2.

First, scope: as written, and read next to the table row at 56, this says a
testbench cannot race a design. It holds only for `program` code.

Second, the argument. Region order alone does not give isolation, and the note is
explicit about why (Part A §2.1, "Isolation — why a program block cannot race the
design"): the guarantee rests on three things together — program processes may
modify design signals only via non-blocking assignments, whose updates land in a
later region; assignments to program variables are blocking but touch only
program variables and so schedule nothing in Active; and all program processes
complete before the Active set is re-entered. That third clause is needed
precisely because the Active set *can* be re-entered in the same timestep, which
the chapter never says (§4.7). "They are not in the same part of the timestep at
all" is not an argument, it is a restatement, and it is the sentence a reader
will try to apply to their own code.

Fix: scope the claim to program code and give the three-part mechanism.

### 4.4 The two-regions-of-nine guarantee overstates what the documentation says (non-blocking)

`chapters/ch04-simulation-fundamentals.qmd:172`

> A tool implementing two regions of nine agrees with the model on any code that
> does not depend on the other seven, and that is most correct code.

The note contradicts this directly (Part C §1.6): the documentation "makes no
blanket promise of equivalence", and three of the documented divergences are
"counterexamples even for code many engineers would call race-free" — the
suppressed initial X→0 negedge, the Design-Compiler-style sensitivity semantics
for `always @(x) y = x & z;` where "a compliant simulator will only calculate y
if x changes", and the conversion of `===`/`!==` to `==`/`!=`. None of those is a
region-ordering question, so "does not depend on the other seven" does not cover
them.

This matters more than a hedge, because the sentence is the reassuring
conclusion of the section and the chapter's own worked example depends on the
opposite being interesting. Fix: keep the useful half — the question is how much
of the model a tool implements, not which category it is in — and replace the
guarantee with the note's own formulation: the divergences are documented at
value level as well as at region level, and determinism across runs is not the
same thing as correctness. `verilator-languages` (`refs.bib:1260`) sources the
correction.

### 4.5 Preponed sampling is stated as observable mechanism (non-blocking)

`chapters/ch04-simulation-fundamentals.qmd:128`

> A concurrent assertion's expression is sampled in Preponed, before anything in
> the timestep has run, so the values it sees are those that were stable going
> into the edge.

Constraint 11 and the note's Part B §3 say the source is explicit that Preponed
and the previous timestep's Postponed are indistinguishable — same values, only
the timestamp differs — and that "it is not observable in which region the
simulator actually samples a value." The chapter does not say "must", so this is
not a constraint violation, but it presents as inspectable mechanism something no
experiment can distinguish, and a reader will go looking.

Fix: lead with the consequence and put the region second — the values a
concurrent assertion evaluates are the ones that were stable going into the edge;
the model places that sampling in Preponed, and no experiment can tell you
whether your simulator sampled there or at the end of the previous timestep. The
narrower claim built on it in the same sentence is fine, and correctly narrow.

### 4.6 The zero-delay loop back-reference promises a different case than it explains (non-blocking)

`chapters/ch04-simulation-fundamentals.qmd:70`

> @sec-ch03 mentioned an `always` block with an empty sensitivity list locking a
> simulator into an infinite loop and did not explain it. Here is the mechanism

`chapters/ch03-digital-design-for-verifiers.qmd:46` does say that, and the
mechanism then given is a different fault: a non-blocking assignment inside a
combinational block retriggering its own sensitivity list (the note's Sutherland
2007 §2.4 case). Both are real and both are worth teaching; they are not the
same, and an empty sensitivity list is left unexplained after being named twice.
Fix: either explain the promised case, or say plainly that this is a second route
to the same symptom.

### 4.7 The timestep is described as a sweep, not a loop (non-blocking)

`chapters/ch04-simulation-fundamentals.qmd:68`

> Work in the Active region can schedule more Active-region work, and the
> simulator keeps going round until nothing is left; only then does it move
> through the later regions, and only when those are empty does time advance.

That is each region iterating internally and then the regions being visited in
order — which is a list of loops, not a loop. The note's structure (Part A §3,
steps 4–5) has a back edge the chapter drops: if the Reactive region set
scheduled anything that can trigger Active-set events, **the Active set
re-triggers and iterates again in the same timestep**, and only when nothing
remains anywhere does Postponed run and time advance.

That back edge is not a curiosity. It is why a testbench driving with zero delay
can cause further combinational activity in the same slot, which is the
sourced argument for driving away from the active edge; it is the clause §4.3
needs; and it is what makes the section heading "Iteration within one timestep"
true of the whole timestep rather than of each region separately. One sentence
fixes it.

## 5. Attribution

### 5.1 A source's distinctive phrase is used unquoted and uncited (blocking)

`chapters/ch04-simulation-fundamentals.qmd:125`

> The one paper devoted to using them calls them surprisingly error-prone

"Surprisingly error-prone" is Bromley and Johnston's own wording — the note
records it as: the construct "has proved to be surprisingly error-prone, despite
nearly a decade of application experience". The chapter borrows the phrase, says
in the same breath that it is quoting a paper, and gives neither quotation marks
nor a citation. The attribution rule allows a sentence or two in quotation marks
with the citation on the same line; it does not allow this. The rest of the
sentence relays their two named failure modes and is uncited too. Fix: quote with
the citation on the same line, or paraphrase from understanding — and cite either
way. See §2.2 for the missing `refs.bib` entry.

### 5.2 A measurement is narrated without naming whose it is (blocking)

`chapters/ch04-simulation-fundamentals.qmd:151`

> the authors add that they had heard fifteen percent claimed and had not seen
> it. They also warn against quoting even their own figure once the tools have
> moved on

"The authors" and "they" have no antecedent in the paragraph, the figures are
specific, and there is no citation. A reader cannot identify the paper, check the
number, or apply the caution the sentence itself endorses. Same fix as §2.1.

### 5.3 The clocking-skew rationale tracks its source closely (non-blocking)

`chapters/ch04-simulation-fundamentals.qmd:122`

> Those skews are not arbitrary conventions. They are where a real flip-flop's
> setup window and clock-to-output delay are

This is Bromley and Johnston §2's argument in their own terms (input skew is the

## 7. Clarity

### 7.1 "Program code" is never explained

`chapters/ch04-simulation-fundamentals.qmd:56` and `:106` both use "program
code", and the chapter never says that `program` is a distinct SystemVerilog
construct with its own scheduling. A first-year DV engineer reads "program code"
as "code in a program" — that is, any code at all — which is exactly the reading
that makes §4.2 and §4.3 land the wrong way. One sentence would fix it: a
`program` block is a testbench container whose processes are scheduled in the
Reactive region set rather than in Active, and code written in an ordinary module
is not one. The chapter defers the construct to Part II, which is fine; it cannot
defer the word it is already using.

### 7.2 "One step" is an undefined quantity

`chapters/ch04-simulation-fundamentals.qmd:128`

> sampling a testbench input one step before the edge is what makes a testbench
> agree with its own assertions

"One step" here is `#1step` — the global time precision, the smallest precision
declared anywhere in the design (note Part B §3 and §5) — and the chapter never
introduces it. As written it reads as "one timestep earlier", which would be a
full cycle of skew and would make the sentence false. Either introduce `#1step`
in a clause, or cut it and keep the consequence. The link to @sec-ch04-time is
worth making, because it is the same quantity: adding one module with a finer
precision changes what `#1step` means everywhere.

### 7.3 Neither simulator is named

`chapters/ch04-simulation-fundamentals.qmd:167` says "one of this book's two
simulators" and "the other", and the run.out labels say "event-driven simulator"
and "statically scheduled simulator", while the citation keys on the same line
name both tools and the example scripts call them by name. A reader who wants to
check 167 against the documentation has to work out which tool is which from the
bibliography. The section is about what specific, named tools document about
themselves; naming them costs nothing and the citations already do it.

### 7.4 The disagreement is shown but not read

`chapters/ch04-simulation-fundamentals.qmd:189`

> one reports a sequence that never settles into a pattern at all

The reader has the numbers directly above — `observed` running 0, 2, 2, 4 against
`count` running 1, 2, 3, 4 — and is left to work out what produced them. One
sentence would carry the whole lesson: the event-driven tool orders the two
processes differently on different edges, so the observer reads sometimes before
and sometimes after the counter's blocking update, and the alternation is what
"arbitrary order within a region" looks like from outside.

### 7.5 The isolation claim at 44 asserts rather than argues

See §4.3. "They are not in the same part of the timestep at all" is the kind of
sentence a reader will quote back at their own testbench, and it will not survive
contact with a module-based one.

### 7.6 A self-aware caution about a source the reader cannot identify

`chapters/ch04-simulation-fundamentals.qmd:151` ends "which is good advice and
applies to this paragraph", referring to a caution issued by authors the
paragraph never names. The self-awareness is the right instinct and reads as
evasion without the citation. Resolved by §2.1.

  already in `refs.bib` as `sutherland2007gotchas`.
- **4.1** — "An `if` whose condition is unknown takes neither branch" is wrong

  wrong and which the chapter itself contradicts at 106.

  completion before Active re-entry.
- **5.1** — "surprisingly error-prone" is the source's phrase, used unquoted and
  uncited.
- **5.2** — a specific measurement narrated with "the authors" and no antecedent
  or citation.

3.2, 3.3, 3.4, 3.5, 4.4, 4.5, 4.6, 4.7, 5.3, 5.4, and all of §6 and §7.

What is working, and should not be disturbed. The constraint discipline is the

chapter and handles guideline 5 exactly as constraint 9 requires. The three-way
